# Step 1 & 2 of Stock Evaluation: Business & Moat Understanding; Industry Structure & Cycle Position

## Step 1a — What is "circle of competence" in practice, and how do investors self-assess it?

### Takeaway
Circle of competence is an operational self-honesty check, not a credential — practitioners test it by (1) taking inventory of what they truly understand, (2) reviewing their own decision track record for where they got it wrong, (3) seeking outside validation, and (4) using an explicit "can I explain this without borrowing someone else's thesis" test before buying.

### Cited Findings
- Warren Buffett's own framing: "The size of the circle is not very important; knowing its boundaries, however, is vital." — [Picture Perfect Portfolios](https://pictureperfectportfolios.com/inside-warren-buffetts-circle-of-competence-concept/)
- Practical self-assessment steps identified across multiple investor-education sources: (1) honest inventory of knowledge/skills/expertise; (2) reviewing one's own track record to see where decisions were consistently good vs. where mistakes clustered; (3) seeking external feedback from colleagues/mentors to confirm actual (not perceived) competence; (4) documenting reasoning and confidence at the time of each decision and reviewing outcomes months later to see if errors clustered outside the stated circle — [Coffee and Junk](https://coffeeandjunk.com/circle-of-competence/); [Jim Bouman](https://jimbouman.com/circle-of-competence/)
- A widely cited practical test: "if you can't explain a company's business model or investment thesis without quoting someone else's opinion, it's probably outside your circle" — [Coffee and Junk](https://coffeeandjunk.com/circle-of-competence/)
- Buffett, Munger, and Lynch are all cited as applying variants of the same discipline — avoiding businesses whose economics they cannot model with confidence rather than avoiding entire sectors dogmatically — [GuruFocus](https://www.gurufocus.com/news/2064642/buffett-munger-and-lynch-on-the-circle-of-competence)
- The circle is explicitly framed as something to be reassessed periodically as the investor's knowledge grows, not a fixed, one-time boundary — [Astute Investors Calculus](https://astuteinvestorscalculus.com/buffetts-circle-of-competence/)

### Inferences
- Operationally, a checklist version of this step would ask: "Can I draw the unit economics / revenue model from memory?", "Can I name the 2-3 things that would make this business fail?", "Have I made money or lost money historically in this exact sector, and why?" — none of these require paid data; they are self-assessment questions a retail investor can run unaided.
- There is no numeric/quantitative threshold for circle of competence anywhere in the literature — it is qualitative self-assessment, which distinguishes it from the moat and industry-structure steps that do have numeric thresholds.

### Gaps
- No academic or industry-standard formal "test" or scoring rubric for circle of competence was found (e.g., no equivalent of an HHI for self-knowledge) — this concept remains advisory/qualitative in all sources reviewed.

## Step 1b — Moat taxonomy: Morningstar's Economic Moat framework and Pat Dorsey's moat sources

### Takeaway
Morningstar rates companies None/Narrow/Wide based on expected duration of excess returns (Narrow = can defend returns ~10 years; Wide = ~20+ years), gated by a quantitative pre-condition (ROIC must exceed WACC), and attributes the moat to five sources: network effect, switching costs, cost advantage, intangible assets, and efficient scale. Pat Dorsey's book (the Morningstar-affiliated "knockout formula") condenses this to four primary sources — intangible assets, switching costs, network effects, cost advantages — treating "efficient scale" as a subtype of cost advantage rather than a fifth independent category.

### Cited Findings
- Morningstar's three ratings and duration test: "If we can expect a company's competitive advantage to last more than 20 years, we consider it as having a wide moat. If it can fend off rivals for 10 years, it has a narrow moat. If a competitive advantage doesn't exist or may prove fleeting, there's no moat." — [Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/stocks/morningstar-economic-moat-rating-3)
- Gating quantitative requirement: to earn a Narrow or Wide rating a firm must have (1) the prospect of earning returns on invested capital above its weighted average cost of capital (ROIC > WACC), and (2) a competitive edge that prevents those returns from quickly eroding — [Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/stocks/morningstar-economic-moat-rating-3)
- Morningstar's five moat sources: intangible assets, switching costs, network effect, cost advantage, and efficient scale — [Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/stocks/morningstar-economic-moat-rating-3)
- Morningstar previously also published a separate "Moat Trend" rating (Positive/Stable/Negative) describing whether the moat is widening or eroding, but retired this as a standalone data point on July 27, 2023 (core Moat Rating methodology unaffected) — [Morningstar: Retiring the Moat Trend Rating](https://www.morningstar.com/stocks/morningstar-is-retiring-moat-trend-rating); [Morningstar: Why Moat Trends Matter](https://www.morningstar.com/portfolios/why-moat-trends-matter)
- Pat Dorsey's "The Little Book That Builds Wealth" (Morningstar's own "knockout formula" book) examines four common sources of economic moats: intangible assets, cost advantages, customer switching costs, and network economics — NOT five; cost advantages are further decomposed into four sub-sources: cheaper processes, better locations, unique assets, and greater scale — [search synthesis of multiple booksellers]; detailed breakdown — [Anomaly Investments: Pat Dorsey — The Moat Framework](https://anomalyinvestments.substack.com/p/pat-dorsey-the-moat-framework)
- Wide-moat companies typically hold multiple reinforcing moat sources simultaneously (e.g., network effect + switching costs + scale economics) rather than relying on a single source — [Anomaly Investments](https://anomalyinvestments.substack.com/p/pat-dorsey-the-moat-framework)

### Inferences
- The "fifth source" discrepancy between Morningstar (5: including efficient scale as independent) and Dorsey (4, folding efficient scale into cost advantage) is a real taxonomic difference, not a research error — Morningstar's current official methodology uses 5; Dorsey's book (written earlier, used as the layperson's introduction to the Morningstar framework) uses 4. A checklist should present Morningstar's 5-category version as the primary taxonomy since it is Morningstar's current live methodology, while crediting Dorsey as the popularizing source for 4 of the 5.
- "Efficient scale" (Morningstar's 5th source) describes a market only big enough to profitably support one or a few players — a moat condition distinct from the "scale cost advantage" Dorsey describes (where a bigger firm simply has lower unit costs); these are related but not identical concepts and both should be kept in a checklist.

## Step 1c — Concrete tests/questions used to detect each moat type in a 10-K or business model

### Takeaway
Each moat type has a specific diagnostic question and an associated financial "tell"; the universal cross-check for all moat types is persistent Return on Capital (ROIC/ROC) above the cost of capital for 10+ years, since moats that are real show up as margins/returns that don't erode to competitive levels over time.

### Cited Findings
- **Network effect test**: Does the value of the service increase as more users join (nonlinear value growth), and can the platform function without broad participation on both sides (e.g., buyers and sellers)? Evidence cited: uncontested network positions can sustain 70%+ EBIT margins (example given: Rightmove); check whether volume/liquidity can migrate away to competitors (example: fragmentation risk in equity trading venues) — [Anomaly Investments](https://anomalyinvestments.substack.com/p/pat-dorsey-the-moat-framework)
- **Switching-cost test**: Is the product/service mission-critical and deeply embedded in daily operations such that downtime or migration would be catastrophic? Specific diagnostic questions: would migrating data create unacceptable risk (e.g., accounting software with years of financial history); does switching require retraining an entire workforce (e.g., CAD/EDA tools); are years of institutional knowledge embedded in the system (e.g., enterprise databases wired into dozens of other systems)? Financial tell: persistence of margins despite competitive pressure and long average customer retention (banking example cited: 6-7 years) — [Anomaly Investments](https://anomalyinvestments.substack.com/p/pat-dorsey-the-moat-framework)
- **Intangible assets (brand) test**: Can the company charge a premium over a functionally equivalent alternative, and does demand structurally exceed supply (e.g., Hermès Birkin waitlists, Tiffany vs. generic diamonds)? Brand recognition alone is explicitly NOT sufficient evidence — the test is demonstrated pricing power, not awareness — [Anomaly Investments](https://anomalyinvestments.substack.com/p/pat-dorsey-the-moat-framework)
- **Intangible assets (patents) test**: A single patent is a "shaky foundation"; look for a diverse patent portfolio plus a consistent multi-decade innovation track record across multiple IP categories — [Anomaly Investments](https://anomalyinvestments.substack.com/p/pat-dorsey-the-moat-framework)
- **Intangible assets (regulatory license) test**: Can new competitors legally enter without government approval, and can the incumbent set prices freely once licensed (examples cited: Moody's 50%+ margins; Verisign's .com registry monopoly)? — [Anomaly Investments](https://anomalyinvestments.substack.com/p/pat-dorsey-the-moat-framework)
- **Cost-advantage test (by sub-source)**: Process (can competitors copy it? compare operating costs as % of revenue — often the most vulnerable/least durable sub-source); Location (transport-cost sensitivity creates geographic mini-monopolies, e.g., cement/gravel pricing can move ~10% within a few miles of plant location); Unique assets (scarcity of comparable resource deposits); Scale (fixed-cost leverage and profit-per-incremental-unit, e.g., UPS: "every additional package is almost pure profit") — [Anomaly Investments](https://anomalyinvestments.substack.com/p/pat-dorsey-the-moat-framework)
- **Universal cross-check**: "A high ROC [return on capital] that persists over time is the primary evidence that a moat is present." Companies without real moats see margins compress toward competitive/industry-average levels over time; genuine moat holders sustain above-average returns for a decade or more — [Anomaly Investments](https://anomalyinvestments.substack.com/p/pat-dorsey-the-moat-framework)
- A "big fish in a small pond" pattern — dominating a niche too small to attract giant competitors — is cited as a durable (if less glamorous) cost-advantage moat pattern, with an example ROC of ~40% (Graco) — [Anomaly Investments](https://anomalyinvestments.substack.com/p/pat-dorsey-the-moat-framework)

### Inferences
- A repeatable checklist should pair each moat-type test with its specific 10-K location: network effects and switching costs are usually described qualitatively in Item 1 (Business) and competitive-risk language in Item 1A (Risk Factors); cost-advantage and scale evidence shows up quantitatively in segment/gross-margin data and capex intensity in the MD&A; intangible-asset/patent evidence shows up in Item 1 (IP strategy, number of patents, license terms) and in R&D expense lines.
- Because brand recognition without pricing power is explicitly called out as a false positive, a checklist item should force the practitioner to find an actual price-premium data point (e.g., compare the company's realized price or gross margin to a generic/private-label competitor) rather than accepting "well-known brand" as sufficient.

- Could not independently verify the S&P Dow Jones Indices "systematic approach for identifying companies with economic moats" research paper (fetch returned HTTP 403 Forbidden) — any additional quantitative screening thresholds S&P may use were not retrievable.
- Could not access the Michael Mauboussin "Measuring the Moat" paper (fetch failed — binary/unreadable PDF stream) — a more rigorous treatment of fade rates and incremental ROIC that is known to exist in this paper could not be confirmed or cited.

## Step 1d — Specific filing evidence that signals moat vs. no moat (margins, market share, customer concentration, R&D%, patents)

### Takeaway
Practitioners look for a specific bundle of 10-K-derivable numbers — sustained ROIC materially above WACC, gross-margin stability (low year-to-year variance) over 5-10 years, customer base diversification, and R&D/patent intensity appropriate to the claimed moat type — and these are almost entirely free to check via SEC EDGAR, unlike market-share/concentration data which typically requires paid industry research.

### Cited Findings
- Core financial indicators of a moat cited across multiple sources: sustained high return on invested capital (ROIC), sustained high gross margins, and high/stable market share — [search synthesis]; one source specifies sustained ROIC above 15% over 5+ years as the threshold where an economic moat becomes "plausible," and ROIC above 25% sustained over a decade as "wide-moat territory" — [Stock Alarm Pro: Economic Moat Investing Guide](https://pro.stockalarm.io/blog/economic-moat-investing-guide)
- Gross-margin stability test: evaluate both the trailing-12-month gross margin level and its 5-year stability; one source proposes a specific bar of standard deviation under 3 percentage points over 5 years as the stability threshold signaling real pricing power — [Stock Alarm Pro](https://pro.stockalarm.io/blog/economic-moat-investing-guide)
- Customer concentration test: one source proposes no single customer should represent more than 15% of revenue as a marker of diversified, lower-risk revenue — [Stock Alarm Pro](https://pro.stockalarm.io/blog/economic-moat-investing-guide)
- Mandatory disclosure trigger retail investors can rely on directly: under ASC 280-10-50-42 (major-customer disclosure) and SEC Regulation S-K Item 101, a public company must disclose when revenue from a single external customer equals or exceeds 10% of total revenue — this is the "bright-line" trigger investors can search for directly in the 10-K's segment/customer-concentration footnotes — [Beancount.io: Customer Concentration Risk — The 10% Rule](https://beancount.io/blog/2026/05/11/customer-concentration-risk-10-percent-revenue-threshold-business-valuation-loan-capacity-negotiating-leverage-guide)
- R&D intensity as a moat signal: R&D spending exceeding roughly 10% of revenue, combined with a measurable, enforceable patent-protection period, is cited as a financial signature consistent with an intangible-asset (brand/patent/license) moat — [Equicurious: Identifying Economic Moats](https://equicurious.com/learn/equities/fundamental-analysis/identifying-economic-moats-and-competitive-advantage)
- Patents as a credibility/quality signal to outside investors: academic research finds that firms receiving initial patent grants subsequently show materially higher growth (one NBER study found patent-grant recipients added ~16 employees and ~$10.6 million in sales over five years, and received 56.5% more subsequent patent grants with 33% higher citations per patent) — though this signaling effect is strongest for smaller/younger firms and weaker for large firms whose R&D is less liquidity-constrained — [NBER Working Paper 19947: Patents as Quality Signals](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w19947/w19947.pdf)
- Strong ROIC, high/stable gross margins, and consistently growing free cash flow are described together as the "financial fingerprints" of a moat, typically found in the 10-K's financial statements and MD&A sections — [search synthesis, Equicurious / Stock Alarm Pro]

### Inferences
- A practical "moat scorecard" a retail investor can build entirely from free SEC EDGAR data: (1) pull 10 years of gross margin and operating margin from the 10-K/10-Q financials and compute the standard deviation; (2) pull ROIC (or approximate via EBIT(1-tax)/(debt+equity-cash)) across the same period and compare to a reasonable WACC estimate; (3) read the major-customer footnote for the 10% disclosure trigger; (4) read Item 1 for patent counts/portfolio breadth and R&D as % of revenue from the income statement.
- Market-share trend (a commonly cited moat signal) is the hardest of these to check for free — companies rarely disclose precise market share in filings (it is usually qualitative language like "leading position"), so verifying actual share trends generally requires paid third-party data (see Step 2 note on data accessibility below).

### Gaps
- No single authoritative, universally-agreed numeric threshold for "gross margin stability" or "customer concentration ceiling" was found in a primary (e.g., Morningstar, CFA Institute) source — the specific numbers above (ROIC>15%/5yr, stdev<3pp, customer<15%) come from a single secondary finance blog (Stock Alarm Pro) rather than a standards body, and should be treated as one practitioner's rule-of-thumb rather than an industry-wide standard.

## Step 2a — Market structure classification: HHI and CR4, with thresholds

### Takeaway
Analysts classify industries from fragmented to monopoly using two main concentration metrics — the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI, sum of squared market shares) and the four-firm concentration ratio (CR4, sum of top-4 shares) — each with widely cited numeric bands, though the HHI thresholds used in U.S. antitrust enforcement were formally lowered in the December 2023 DOJ/FTC Merger Guidelines.

### Cited Findings
- HHI formula: sum of the squares of all competitors' market shares in a relevant market — [Hall Render: FTC and DOJ Release Finalized Merger Guidelines](https://hallrender.com/2023/12/27/ftc-and-doj-release-finalized-merger-guidelines/)
- **Current (Dec. 18, 2023) DOJ/FTC Merger Guidelines**: a merger is presumed unlawful if, post-merger, either (1) HHI > 1,800 AND the change in HHI > 100 points, or (2) the resulting firm's market share > 30% AND the change in HHI > 100 points — [Hall Render](https://hallrender.com/2023/12/27/ftc-and-doj-release-finalized-merger-guidelines/)
- This 1,800 HHI presumption threshold is a 700-point decrease from the prior (2010) Horizontal Merger Guidelines' 2,500 threshold, and the "change in HHI" trigger was cut from 200 points to 100 points; the 30%-market-share trigger is new in 2023 and did not exist in prior guidelines — [Hall Render](https://hallrender.com/2023/12/27/ftc-and-doj-release-finalized-merger-guidelines/)
- **Prior / commonly-taught HHI bands** (from the 2010 Horizontal Merger Guidelines, still widely used in finance education/classification even though superseded for enforcement purposes): Unconcentrated market = HHI below 1,500; Moderately concentrated = HHI 1,500–2,500; Highly concentrated = HHI above 2,500 — [Pearson: Mergers and the HHI Explained](https://www.pearson.com/channels/microeconomics/learn/brian/ch-12-monopoly/mergers-and-the-herfindahl-hirschman-index-hhi)
- **CR4 (four-firm concentration ratio)**: CR4 = sum of the market shares of the four largest firms. Commonly cited bands: CR4 < 40% = fragmented market (no small group dominates, robust competition); CR4 40–60% = "loose oligopoly" (early signs of a few firms having enough combined weight to influence pricing); CR4 > 60% = "tight oligopoly" (four or fewer firms control the majority, limited real competition) — [search synthesis of multiple sources, incl. LegalClarity: Four-Firm Concentration Ratio](https://legalclarity.org/how-to-calculate-the-four-firm-concentration-ratio-cr4/)
- CR4 limitation: it is typically calculated on domestic revenue/output data, so in industries with significant import competition a high domestic CR4 (e.g., 85%) can overstate actual pricing power if foreign suppliers meet a large share of real demand (e.g., 30%) — [LegalClarity](https://legalclarity.org/how-to-calculate-the-four-firm-concentration-ratio-cr4/)
- **Qualitative market-structure labels and rough thresholds**: Monopoly — a single firm generally defined as holding more than ~25% of market share in the loosest definitions (true monopoly = ~100% share, one seller); Duopoly — market dominated by two firms, with most real-world duopolies seeing the two largest firms control more than 70% of combined share; Oligopoly — no single fixed firm-count threshold, but an industry dominated by a few firms typically shows a five-firm concentration ratio (CR5) above 50% — [Economics Online: What Is a Duopoly?](https://www.economicsonline.co.uk/competitive_markets/what-is-a-duopoly.html/); [Economics Help: Duopoly](https://www.economicshelp.org/blog/glossary/duopoly/); [St. Louis Fed: What Makes a Market an Oligopoly?](https://www.stlouisfed.org/open-vault/2023/may/what-makes-a-market-an-oligopoly)
- Fragmented market is defined by exclusion/contrast — many firms, no dominant small group, the opposite end of the spectrum from oligopoly/monopoly — [search synthesis; no single primary numeric definition found]

### Inferences
- For a repeatable checklist, use CR4 bands (fragmented <40%, loose oligopoly 40-60%, tight oligopoly >60%) as the primary practical classifier since CR4 is easier for a retail investor to approximate (sum of 4 largest known competitors' revenue ÷ total industry revenue) than true HHI, which requires every firm's share, including the long tail.
- Because the "monopoly/duopoly" numeric definitions (25% share, 70% combined) come from general economics-education sources rather than a single authoritative body (unlike HHI/CR4, which trace to DOJ/FTC and standard IO economics respectively), they should be flagged in any checklist as informal/rule-of-thumb rather than regulatory thresholds.
- Note the direct conflict/supersession between the 2010 HHI bands (1500/2500, still widely taught) and the 2023 DOJ/FTC enforcement threshold (1800) — a checklist should present both, labeled by purpose: 1500/2500 for classroom/classification language, 1800+30%-share for current actual U.S. merger-enforcement risk.

### Gaps
- Could not find a single primary citation (e.g., directly from the DOJ/FTC merger guidelines text itself) restating the 1500/2500 unconcentrated/moderate/highly-concentrated bands in the same breath as the new 1800 threshold — the 1500/2500 figures are sourced to a finance-education site (Pearson) rather than the original 2010 guideline document, which should be verified against the primary source if higher confidence is required.
- No authoritative numeric source was found for where exactly "fragmented" begins on the CR4 scale (the <40% figure is a commonly repeated rule of thumb across non-primary sources, not from a government or academic body).

## Step 2b — Secular growth vs. cyclical industries: how practitioners distinguish them

### Takeaway
The standard practitioner test is correlation of industry revenue/EPS with the broader business cycle (GDP, or a sector-specific demand driver) — cyclical industries show revenue that rises in expansions and falls sharply in contractions, while secular-growth industries are driven by structural changes in customer behavior that are largely independent of where the economy is in its cycle.

### Cited Findings
- Core distinction: "A cyclical industry refers to an industry whose revenue generation capabilities are tied to the business cycle, meaning its performance is correlated to the business cycle," versus secular growth, which "occurs when something fundamentally changes within a sector or industry, creating a wave of new demand" that depends on changes in customer behavior rather than changes to GDP — [Osterweis Capital Management: Why Secular Growth Matters](https://www.osterweis.com/insights/finding_secular_growth); [Corporate Finance Institute: Cyclical Industry](https://corporatefinanceinstitute.com/resources/economics/cyclical-industry/)
- Practical test cited: analysts evaluate cyclicality primarily "by analyzing top-line revenue from financial statements, as industries that experience greater volatility in their revenue are likely to be more cyclical" — [Wall Street Mojo: Cyclical Industry](https://www.wallstreetmojo.com/cyclical-industry/)
- Cyclical industries "deliver positive returns during expanding economies and do poorly during a downturn," while secular growth rates "can be materially higher than cyclical growth rates" because they are structurally, not cyclically, driven — [Corporate Finance Institute](https://corporatefinanceinstitute.com/resources/economics/cyclical-industry/)
- Commonly cited cyclical industries: auto components, construction, semiconductors, steel, airlines, hotels, restaurants/leisure, textiles/apparel, luxury goods — [Wall Street Mojo](https://www.wallstreetmojo.com/cyclical-industry/)

### Inferences
- The "peak-to-trough drawdown" and "capital intensity" tests mentioned in the task's key questions are logical corollaries of the revenue-volatility test above (more revenue volatility tends to coincide with higher capital intensity and deeper historical drawdowns in cyclical sectors like steel, airlines, and semiconductors), but no source found gave a specific numeric drawdown or capital-intensity threshold distinguishing "cyclical" from "secular" — this remains a comparative/relative judgment (compare the company's own 10-year revenue drawdown and capex/revenue ratio to sector peers) rather than an absolute cutoff.
- A retail-checkable version of this test: pull 10-15 years of quarterly or annual revenue/EPS from SEC filings, overlay against recession dates (NBER-dated, free and public), and visually/statistically check whether revenue troughs line up with recessions — this requires no paid data.

### Gaps
- No source found gives a specific correlation-coefficient threshold (e.g., "correlation with GDP growth above X = cyclical") — the literature describes the concept qualitatively/comparatively rather than with a hard statistical cutoff.
- No specific peak-to-trough drawdown percentage threshold was found distinguishing cyclical from secular industries.

## Step 2c — "Where are we in the cycle" indicators for cyclical industries

### Takeaway
Practitioners triangulate cycle position using a mosaic of indicators rather than one number: broad macro gauges (ISM Manufacturing PMI, capacity utilization, GDP/credit data) combined with industry-specific leading indicators (e.g., the SEMI book-to-bill ratio for semiconductors), plus company-level signals like inventory levels and customer capex guidance.

### Cited Findings
- **ISM Manufacturing PMI**: a monthly survey-based index; its overall reading is "the equally weighted summation of diffusion indexes of five... indicators: new orders, production, employment, supplier delivery, and inventory." September 2026 reading was 54.5%, with New Orders Index at 55.3% (up from 53.7% in August) and the Inventories Index at 48.6% (down from 50.6% in August) with Customers' Inventories remaining in "too low" territory — [ISM: September 2026 Manufacturing PMI Report](https://www.ismworld.org/supply-management-news-and-reports/reports/ism-pmi-reports/pmi/september/)
- **Capacity utilization**: ratio of actual output to sustainable maximum output; "rises and falls with business cycles" — drops during recessions as demand weakens, rises in strong economies as businesses increase production to meet demand. The U.S. Federal Reserve monitors capacity utilization across 89 industries in manufacturing, mining, and utilities (the G.17 release); the long-run U.S. national average typically runs 75-80%, while 80-85% is cited as the "optimal" operating range for most manufacturers/distributors (balancing efficiency against flexibility for maintenance/demand surges) — [Federal Reserve G.17 Release Notes](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g17/capnotes.htm); [Qoblex: What Is Capacity Utilization?](https://qoblex.com/blog/what-is-capacity-utilization-definition-formula-how-to-calculate-it/)
- **Semiconductor book-to-bill ratio (industry-specific example indicator)**: ratio of 3-month moving average of bookings (new orders) to 3-month moving average of shipments (billings) for the North American semiconductor equipment industry, published by SEMI; a ratio above 1.0 means more orders were received than filled (strong demand signal), below 1.0 means weak demand (e.g., 0.98 = $98 of orders received per $100 billed); it is explicitly described as "an important leading indicator of demand trends" and is closely watched by semiconductor analysts and investors; SEMI publishes this approximately three weeks after each month's close — [Wikipedia: Book-to-bill ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book-to-bill_ratio); [SEMI: September 2016 Book-to-Bill Ratio](https://www.semi.org/en/september-2016-book-bill-ratio); [Investing.com: US SEMI Book/Bill Ratio](https://www.investing.com/economic-calendar/united-states-semi-book-bill-ratio-1512)
- Note: the SEMI book-to-bill ratio tracks orders/shipments of semiconductor manufacturing *equipment* (the tools used to make chips), not shipments of the chips themselves — an important distinction when using it as a proxy for chip-industry demand (it is actually more directly a capex/upstream-equipment cycle indicator) — [Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/economic-calendar/united-states-semi-book-bill-ratio-1512)
- Howard Marks' "Mastering the Market Cycle" treats cycle position as a mosaic read across many partial indicators — GDP growth, employment data, inflation, central bank policy, credit availability, valuation levels (P/E ratios), bond yields, and investor sentiment — with the explicit discipline being to read across the mosaic rather than rely on one "magic data point"; his cycle framework runs through phases such as recovery → expansion → euphoria → collapse → despair, and the practical investment rule is: high in the cycle = emphasize loss avoidance (risk is elevated, prospective returns low); low in the cycle = emphasize not missing opportunity (prospective returns high, risk lower) — [Picture Perfect Portfolios: How to Invest Like Howard Marks](https://pictureperfectportfolios.com/how-to-invest-like-howard-marks-mastering-market-cycles/); [FinanceHobbyist: Market Cycles According to Howard Marks](https://www.financehobbyist.com/p/market-cycles-according-to-howard); primary source presentation (title confirms authorship, content not independently re-verified by fetch): [Howard Marks / CalPERS: Mastering the Market Cycle slides](https://www.calpers.ca.gov/documents/201901-full-day1-05-howard-marks-pp-a/download)

### Inferences
- All of the macro/industry indicators cited here (ISM PMI, Fed capacity utilization, SEMI book-to-bill) are published free and publicly — fully retail-checkable without a paid data subscription. This contrasts with company-specific customer-capex-guidance tracking (mentioned in the task's key questions), which requires manually reading earnings call transcripts/investor-day commentary across a company's major customers — free in principle (via SEC filings/transcripts) but labor-intensive, and often faster via paid aggregators (e.g., AlphaSense, Bloomberg) in practice.
- A sector-agnostic version of the semiconductor book-to-bill concept exists for other industries too (e.g., Class I railroad traffic data, housing starts/permits for homebuilders, same-store sales for retail) — the task's example (semiconductors) is one instance of a general pattern: every durable cyclical industry tends to have its own free, publicly tracked order-flow or utilization proxy.

### Gaps
- Could not independently verify the full content of Howard Marks' own CalPERS presentation PDF (not fetched/parsed in this research pass) — the cycle-phase and indicator claims above rely on third-party summaries of the book/talk rather than the primary document text itself; a report relying heavily on exact phrasing from Marks should seek to verify against the book directly.
- No specific numeric threshold (e.g., "PMI above X and capacity utilization above Y = late-cycle") combining multiple indicators into a single "innings of the cycle" score was found in any source — Marks' own framework is explicitly against mechanical threshold-based cycle timing, favoring qualitative mosaic judgment instead.

## Named investors/frameworks for Steps 1 and 2

### Takeaway
Five named frameworks recur across the literature for these two steps: Morningstar's Economic Moat Rating (quantitative + taxonomic moat methodology), Pat Dorsey's four/five-source moat framework (the popularized, checklist-friendly version of Morningstar's methodology), Michael Porter's Five Forces (industry structural attractiveness), Howard Marks' cycle-position mosaic framework (Oaktree/"Mastering the Market Cycle"), and Peter Lynch's six-category stock classification (which operationalizes "what kind of business/cycle position is this" into an investment-decision framework).

### Cited Findings
- **Morningstar Economic Moat Rating**: None/Narrow/Wide ratings gated by ROIC>WACC plus one or more of 5 moat sources (network effect, switching costs, cost advantage, intangible assets, efficient scale); duration-based definition (Narrow ≈10yr, Wide ≈20+yr) — [Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/stocks/morningstar-economic-moat-rating-3)
- **Pat Dorsey**, "The Little Book That Builds Wealth" (Morningstar-affiliated): popularized 4-source version (intangible assets, switching costs, network effects, cost advantages, the last split into process/location/unique-assets/scale sub-sources); universal test = persistent ROC — [Anomaly Investments](https://anomalyinvestments.substack.com/p/pat-dorsey-the-moat-framework)
- **Michael Porter's Five Forces**: threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, competitive rivalry among existing firms, bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of buyers; originally a 1979/2008 Harvard Business Review strategy framework, now also described as "the most widely used tool for industry analysis in... investment research," applied by equity analysts via a qualitative checklist of questions under each force (e.g., under "threat of new entrants": has there been a notable influx of new entrants recently, and do network effects exist in the industry); commercial industry-research providers (IBISWorld, MarketLine) structure their industry reports around this same framework — [Wikipedia: Porter's Five Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porter's_five_forces_analysis); [checklist-approach description, aggregated from Baruch College guide and valuation-education sources](https://valuationmasterclass.com/porters-five-forces/)
- **Howard Marks (Oaktree Capital)**, "Mastering the Market Cycle": cycle position read as a mosaic across many partial macro/market indicators (not one magic number); phases progress through recovery/expansion/euphoria/collapse/despair; the central actionable rule is to shift the portfolio's posture (defense vs. aggression) based on roughly where the mosaic places you in the cycle — [Picture Perfect Portfolios](https://pictureperfectportfolios.com/how-to-invest-like-howard-marks-mastering-market-cycles/); primary presentation — [CalPERS / Howard Marks slides](https://www.calpers.ca.gov/documents/201901-full-day1-05-howard-marks-pp-a/download)
- **Peter Lynch** ("One Up on Wall Street" / "Beating the Street"): classifies stocks into six categories — Slow Growers (mature, dividend-oriented, grow ~in line with inflation/population), Stalwarts (large companies growing ~10-12%/year, e.g., Coca-Cola, Procter & Gamble, Bristol-Myers), Fast Growers (20%+ annual growth), Cyclicals (tied to economic cycle), Turnarounds (distressed businesses recovering), and Asset Plays (hidden/underappreciated balance-sheet value) — the explicit purpose of the taxonomy is to align the investor's expected holding period, valuation approach, and risk tolerance to the type of business before buying — [Picture Perfect Portfolios: Peter Lynch's Six Stock Categories](https://pictureperfectportfolios.com/peter-lynch-six-stock-categories/); [Thesis Rationale: Super Investor Series — Peter Lynch](https://thesisrationale.substack.com/p/super-investor-series-peter-lynch)

### Inferences
- Lynch's taxonomy is effectively the output layer that synthesizes Steps 1 and 2: "Cyclical" as a Lynch category directly maps to the Step 2 cycle-position work; "Stalwart"/"Slow Grower" vs. "Fast Grower" maps to the secular-vs-cyclical and moat-durability findings from Step 1. A combined checklist could explicitly route the Step 1/2 findings into a Lynch category as the final classification output before moving to valuation.
- Porter's Five Forces and Morningstar's Moat framework substantially overlap (e.g., "threat of new entrants" ≈ inverse of moat strength broadly, "bargaining power of suppliers/buyers" overlaps with switching-cost and scale analysis) but are not identical: Porter's framework is industry-level and forward/structural (will this industry stay attractive), while Morningstar/Dorsey's moat framework is firm-level and evidence-based (does this specific company have a demonstrated edge). A rigorous checklist should run Porter's Five Forces at the industry level (Step 2) and the Moat taxonomy at the company level (Step 1), rather than conflating them.

### Gaps
- None of the sources found provided a direct, explicit cross-reference connecting all five frameworks into one unified published checklist (e.g., no single primary source maps Lynch's six categories directly onto Morningstar moat ratings or Porter's five forces) — the synthesis of how these frameworks relate to each other above is this research's own inference, not a cited claim from a single source, and should be flagged as such to the report writer.

## Retail-checkable vs. paid-data-required — cross-cutting summary

### Takeaway
Nearly all of Step 1's financial-evidence checks (margins, ROIC, customer concentration, R&D%, patents) and most of Step 2's macro/cycle indicators (PMI, capacity utilization, book-to-bill) are free and retail-checkable via SEC EDGAR, the Federal Reserve, ISM, and SEMI; the hardest things to verify without paid data are precise market-share/HHI/CR4 calculations for a full industry (since the long tail of smaller competitors' revenue is rarely public) and systematic cross-company comparisons (e.g., peer benchmarking screens), which typically require commercial data providers (IBISWorld, Euromonitor, S&P Capital IQ, Bloomberg, Gartner).

### Cited Findings
- 10-K-derived moat metrics (gross margin history, ROIC inputs, major-customer 10% disclosure, R&D as % of revenue, patent counts in Item 1) are all sourced directly from mandatory SEC disclosures (Regulation S-K Item 101, ASC 280-10-50-42, ASC 275-10-50-16 through 50-20) that are free on SEC EDGAR — [Beancount.io](https://beancount.io/blog/2026/05/11/customer-concentration-risk-10-percent-revenue-threshold-business-valuation-loan-capacity-negotiating-leverage-guide)
- ISM Manufacturing PMI reports, Federal Reserve G.17 capacity-utilization data, and SEMI's book-to-bill ratio are all published free to the public on a recurring monthly schedule — [ISM](https://www.ismworld.org/supply-management-news-and-reports/reports/ism-pmi-reports/pmi/september/); [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g17/capnotes.htm); [SEMI](https://www.semi.org/en/september-2016-book-bill-ratio)
- By contrast, exact HHI calculation requires every competitor's market share in a defined relevant market — a figure companies virtually never disclose precisely in 10-Ks (only qualitative language like "leading position" or "one of the top three providers") — meaning precise HHI typically requires a paid industry-research subscription (sources like IBISWorld were referenced as a typical provider of this kind of industry structure data in the equity-research literature reviewed above) — [search synthesis; no single primary source directly states "retail investors cannot get HHI for free," this is this research's own inference from the absence of free sources found]

### Inferences
- A practical compromise for a retail-built checklist: use CR4 approximated from the largest public competitors' disclosed segment revenue (often doable from 10-Ks of the 3-4 largest listed players in a space) as a "good enough" substitute for true HHI, explicitly flagging it as an approximation that excludes private/foreign competitors.

### Gaps
- No source was found that explicitly benchmarks "how much it costs" or "which specific paid provider" to get precise HHI/CR4 data for a given industry — this inference is based on the absence of any free, comprehensive market-share database being surfaced in this research, not a direct citation stating paid data is required.
