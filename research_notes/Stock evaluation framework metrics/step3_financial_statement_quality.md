# Step 3: Financial Statement Quality — Metrics, Formulas, Thresholds, Sector Variance

## Key Question 1: Revenue growth quality — distinguishing organic growth from acquisition/FX/one-time inflated growth

### Takeaway
"Organic revenue growth" is a non-GAAP measure companies voluntarily reconcile in MD&A/10-K footnotes by stripping out FX translation effects, acquisitions/divestitures, and other one-time items from reported (GAAP) revenue growth; there is no single standardized SEC-mandated format, so the specific adjustments vary by issuer and must be read company-by-company.

### Cited Findings
- Organic revenue growth is supplemental, non-GAAP information meant to help investors evaluate growth from existing operations; it typically "includes the impact of intersegment and intrasegment activity and excludes the impact of foreign exchange rate changes, acquisitions, divestitures, transfers between business units, fiduciary investment income, reimbursable expenses, and certain unusual items" — [Aon plc 10-K filings, SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000315293/000162828017001652/aonplc201610-k.htm)
- Companies provide reconciliation tables in their 10-Ks that walk from reported (GAAP) revenue growth → adjustments for currency impact, acquisitions/divestitures, fiduciary income, etc. → organic revenue growth (non-GAAP) — [Aon plc 10-K, SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/315293/000162828020001607/aonplc201910-k.htm); similar GAAP/Non-GAAP reconciliation format also seen in [Q4 2025 Reconciliation of Financial Information](https://s27.q4cdn.com/797047529/files/doc_financials/2025/q4/Q4-2025-Reconciliation-of-Financial-Information.pdf)
- SEC rules require companies to explicitly disclose that organic/non-GAAP revenue growth measures are not prepared in accordance with U.S. GAAP and should be viewed in addition to (not instead of) the Consolidated Financial Statements and Notes; peer companies may make different adjustments, limiting cross-company comparability — [Aon plc 10-K, SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/315293/000162828026008116/aon-20251231.htm)

### Inferences
- A repeatable checklist should (1) pull reported revenue growth from the income statement, (2) search the MD&A/non-GAAP reconciliation section (often titled "Non-GAAP Financial Measures" or "Reconciliation of Non-GAAP Measures") for a company-provided organic growth bridge, (3) separately check the business combinations/acquisitions footnote for "contribution of acquisitions to revenue" disclosures (required under ASC 805) when no organic growth table is given, and (4) check the "Quantitative and Qualitative Disclosures About Market Risk" or segment footnotes for FX translation impact on revenue when a company discloses revenue growth "in constant currency."
- Because the adjustments are issuer-defined and non-GAAP, a per-ticker checklist should flag whenever a company (a) provides no organic growth reconciliation at all (lower transparency) or (b) changes its definition/adjustments year over year (a potential earnings-quality flag in itself).

### Gaps
- No specific worked numerical example of an organic-growth reconciliation table (line-by-line) was retrieved in full; only the general structure/definition was confirmed. A report writer wanting an exact table should pull one directly from a specific company's 10-K (e.g., Aon plc's MD&A) rather than relying on summarized search snippets.
- No single authoritative source (e.g., SEC rule citation number for the non-GAAP disclosure requirement, likely Regulation G / Item 10(e) of Regulation S-K) was directly fetched; this should be verified against SEC.gov Regulation G text if precision on the legal requirement is needed.

## Key Question 2: Margin trends — gross/operating/net margin formulas and sector-varying "healthy" levels

### Takeaway
Gross margin = (Revenue − COGS) / Revenue; Operating margin = Operating Income (EBIT) / Revenue; Net margin = Net Income / Revenue — and "healthy" absolute levels for all three vary enormously by sector, so trend direction (3/5/10-yr) and sector-relative positioning matter more than any universal numeric bar; banks do not use these margin metrics at all and instead rely on Net Interest Margin and efficiency ratio.

### Cited Findings
- **Software**: System & Application software gross margin ≈71.7%; packaged software typically 70–85% gross margin; SaaS specifically 70–80% gross margin — [Vena / industry benchmark aggregation via search](https://www.venasolutions.com/blog/average-profit-margin-by-industry); software net margin benchmark cited at 25.49% for system & application software, but SaaS median net margin cited at just 12% (with top-quartile SaaS >25%, and high-growth SaaS names running near breakeven) — [Net Profit Margin by Industry (2026)](https://www.calcmastery.com/benchmarks/net-profit-margin-by-industry/); [EagleRockCFO margin guide](https://eaglerockcfo.com/blog/profitability-guide/gross-margin-benchmarks). **Note: these two net-margin figures for software conflict significantly (25.5% vs. 12% median) — likely reflecting different sub-segment definitions (enterprise system software vs. broad SaaS universe including unprofitable growth names); treat as a range, not a point estimate.**
- **Semiconductors**: gross margin ≈55–59% (58.97% in one aggregation, 55.05% in a more recent Q2 2026 csimarket read); operating margin 48.48% and net margin 41.02% (csimarket, Q2 2026) vs. a separate net margin benchmark of 30.45% cited elsewhere — [csimarket Semiconductors Industry Ratios](https://csimarket.com/Industry/industry_Profitability_Ratios.php?ind=1010); [Net Profit Margin by Industry](https://www.calcmastery.com/benchmarks/net-profit-margin-by-industry/). **Note: semiconductor net margin figures also conflict (41% vs 30.5%) — again likely cyclical-timing or sub-segment differences (fabless vs IDM vs equipment); flag as cyclical and point-in-time sensitive.**
- **Retail**: gross margin ranges cited both as ~33.18% (general retail) / 26.31% (grocery/food retail) in one source, and 35–55% in another; operating margin 4–10%; net margin 3–7%; "even at 50% gross margin, retail operating margins rarely exceed 12% because of occupancy, labor, and inventory costs" — [Vena industry benchmarks](https://www.venasolutions.com/blog/average-profit-margin-by-industry); [Net Profit Margin by Industry (2026)](https://www.calcmastery.com/benchmarks/net-profit-margin-by-industry/). **Note: the two retail gross-margin ranges (33%/26% vs 35-55%) do not fully agree — grocery/discount retail sits at the low end, specialty/apparel retail at the high end, so sub-sector matters.**
- **Industrials**: median net margin ≈8%, top quartile ≈14%; aerospace & defense and precision instruments can reach 12–18% net margin, while freight, construction, and cyclical machinery compress to low single digits — [Net Profit Margin by Industry (2026)](https://www.calcmastery.com/benchmarks/net-profit-margin-by-industry/)
- **Banks** do not use gross/operating/net margin on revenue; they use **Net Interest Margin (NIM)** instead, which typically ranges 2–5% overall, with community banks at 3.5–4.5%, regional banks at 3.0–3.75%, and large national banks at 2.0–3.0% (larger balance sheets and more diversified/low-yield assets compress NIM) — [banksift.org NIM FAQ](https://banksift.org/faq/what-is-a-good-nim-for-banks); banks also use **efficiency ratio** (roughly: non-interest expense / (net interest income + non-interest income)), where below 60% is an optimal target, 50–60% is well-run, below 50% is "exceptional," and benchmarks scale with size: large national banks target 55–60%, regional banks 60–65%, community banks 65–70% — [timvero.com Bank Efficiency Ratio](https://timvero.com/blog/bank-efficiency-ratio)
- General principle: "The right benchmark is always the sector median — comparing a retailer's 25% margin to a SaaS company's 75% margin is inappropriate" — [basisreport.com Gross Margin by Industry Sector](https://www.basisreport.com/resources/gross-margin-by-industry-sector-benchmarks)

### Inferences
- A per-ticker checklist should pull 3/5/10-year gross, operating, and net margin as a time series (not point-in-time) and classify trend direction (expanding / flat / contracting) separately from absolute level-vs-sector-peer positioning — a company can have a "low" sector-relative margin but a healthy improving trend (or vice versa, a "high" margin that is steadily eroding, which is the more worrying signal).
- Given the conflicting benchmark figures found above (software net margin 12% vs 25.5%; semiconductor net margin 30.5% vs 41%; retail gross margin 26-33% vs 35-55%), any checklist that hardcodes a single "healthy margin" number per sector should instead store a range and cite its source, and prefer comparing the target company against a basket of direct named peers over a generic "industry average" figure, since industry aggregators disagree with each other.
- For banks, a stock-quality checklist needs a parallel branch of metrics entirely (NIM + efficiency ratio + ROA/ROE, see Q3) rather than trying to force gross/operating margin onto bank filings.

### Gaps
- No utilities-specific gross/operating/net margin benchmark was retrieved in this pass (utilities margin structure is dominated by regulated-return mechanics rather than conventional cost margins); a follow-up search specifically on "utility operating margin regulated rate base" would be needed to fill this gap.
- Exact formula sourcing for operating margin (whether "operating income" should be GAAP operating income or adjusted/non-GAAP operating income, which differs significantly for companies with heavy SBC or restructuring charges) was not explicitly addressed in sources found; this nuance should be flagged in the checklist (i.e., always check if the operating margin being compared is GAAP or adjusted).

## Key Question 3: ROE vs ROIC — formulas, why ROE misleads, worked example, and the ROIC-vs-WACC value-creation threshold

### Takeaway
ROE = Net Income / (Average) Shareholders' Equity, while ROIC = NOPAT / Average Invested Capital (NOPAT = EBIT × (1 − tax rate); Invested Capital = Debt + Equity − excess cash, i.e., operating assets financed by all capital providers); ROE is structurally distorted by leverage and buybacks because it divides by equity alone after interest expense, while ROIC is capital-structure-neutral because it divides by total capital before interest expense — so ROIC can stay flat (or even reveal deterioration) while financial engineering pushes ROE higher. A sustained ROIC–WACC spread of roughly 2–3 percentage points is considered a meaningfully positive signal, and a spread sustained above ~5 points is viewed as strong/clearly value-creating, while ROIC below WACC signals value destruction regardless of ROE.

### Cited Findings
- ROIC formula: **ROIC = NOPAT ÷ Average Invested Capital**; **NOPAT = EBIT × (1 − Tax Rate %)**, described as "EBIAT" (earnings before interest, after taxes) — the tax-affected operating income representing cash earnings available to all capital providers (debt + equity) if the company had no debt — [WallStreetPrep, ROIC](https://www.wallstreetprep.com/knowledge/roic-return-on-invested-capital/)
- Invested Capital = sum of fixed assets, net working capital (NWC), and acquired intangibles/goodwill; equivalently, Invested Capital = Debt + Equity − excess cash — [search aggregation citing WallStreetPrep/breakingintowallstreet methodology](https://breakingintowallstreet.com/kb/financial-statement-analysis/roic-return-on-invested-capital/)
- Greenblatt's Magic Formula version of ROIC uses **EBIT ÷ (Net Fixed Assets + Working Capital)** rather than NOPAT/total invested capital — he deliberately uses pre-tax EBIT (not NOPAT) and tangible capital employed (net working capital + net fixed assets, excluding goodwill) "to put companies with different debt levels and tax rates on an equal footing when comparing returns" — [oldschoolvalue.com, The Reasoning Behind Return On Capital in the Magic Formula](https://www.oldschoolvalue.com/stock-valuation/the-magic-formula-return-on-capital/); [einvestingforbeginners.com](https://einvestingforbeginners.com/return-on-capital-daah/)
- ROE formula: **ROE = Net Income ÷ Average Shareholders' Equity**; DuPont decomposition: **ROE = Net Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier** (Equity Multiplier = Total Assets ÷ Common Equity, i.e., the leverage term) — [WallStreetPrep, Return on Equity](https://www.wallstreetprep.com/knowledge/return-on-equity-roe/)
- Mechanism — buybacks: "Stock buybacks can cause shareholders' equity balance to decrease – and the lower denominator can cause an 'artificial' increase in ROE," since ROE = NI/Average Equity, so shrinking equity with no change in net income mathematically raises the ratio — [WallStreetPrep, ROE](https://www.wallstreetprep.com/knowledge/return-on-equity-roe/)
- Cited numeric example #1 (small company): "A company with $1 million in net income and $5 million in equity has an ROE of 20%. If it takes on debt to buy back shares and reduces equity to $2 million, ROE jumps to 50% — not because it's earning more, but because the denominator shrank." — [search aggregation of ROE sources](https://www.supermoney.com/encyclopedia/return-on-equity)
- Cited numeric example #2 (larger company): "A company repurchases $200m; reported equity falls from $800m to $600m. If net income stays $60m, reported ROE jumps from 7.5% to 10%, while buyback-adjusted ROE using original equity is 7.5%." — [search aggregation of ROE sources](https://www.wallstreetprep.com/knowledge/return-on-equity-roe/)
- Cited numeric example #3 (WallStreetPrep template, 5-year buyback company): Year 1 Net Income $20M on average equity → ROE 15.9%; Year 5 Net Income $28M → ROE 20.2%, with $110M of accumulated treasury stock by Year 5 and total shareholders' equity of $140M vs. a non-buyback comparison company's $230M equity for the same operating net-income growth path. The source states this ROE improvement came "solely because equity shrank through buybacks, not from operational efficiency improvements," despite identical underlying operating net income growth ($20M→$28M) in both companies compared — [WallStreetPrep, Return on Equity](https://www.wallstreetprep.com/knowledge/return-on-equity-roe/)
- Leverage mechanism: "A company that loads up on debt shrinks its equity base — which mathematically pushes ROE higher even if profitability hasn't improved." Financial leverage increases ROE by using debt to finance operations, potentially amplifying the return on the (smaller) equity base without any gain in operating profitability — [search aggregation](https://dcf-model.com/blogs/blog/examining-limitations-roe)
- ROIC vs. WACC threshold: "WACC is the threshold of return a company must meet to create value. A business only creates value when ROIC is higher than WACC... If ROIC is equal to WACC, a $100 investment is worth $100... Falling below WACC over time indicates value destruction." A positive ROIC−WACC spread of **at least 200–300 basis points (2–3%) is considered strong**, and "a sustained ROIC-WACC spread of over 5% is preferred." If ROIC > WACC, growth increases value; if ROIC < WACC, growth destroys value — [dividend.school, ROIC vs WACC](https://www.dividend.school/p/roic-vs-wacc-how-value-creation-really); [basisreport.com ROIC vs WACC spread calculator](https://www.basisreport.com/tools/roic-calculator)
- A more qualitative framing (StockUnlock, discussing Damodaran-style reasoning): a small positive ROIC−WACC spread is "insufficient evidence of value creation given WACC estimation uncertainty," while "much stronger evidence comes from a company earning returns comfortably above both WACC and its relevant peers for years" — context (peer comparison, persistence) matters more than a single fixed cutoff — [stockunlock.com, What Is a Good ROIC?](https://stockunlock.com/investing/what-is-a-good-roic/)
- Damodaran-sourced ROIC and WACC levels (recent data per search aggregation, sourced to NYU Stern's January data updates): "in January 2026, General Utilities showed a ROIC of roughly 6.0% against a 4.36% cost of capital, while Internet Software was around 6.2% with an estimated cost of capital of 10.66%" — i.e., the Internet Software sector's ROIC (6.2%) was actually *below* its WACC (10.66%), a value-destructive spread, while Utilities had a modest positive spread (6.0% vs 4.36%) — [search aggregation referencing Damodaran NYU Stern Data Updates](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/blog/DataUpdate1for2023.pdf). **Flag: this specific Internet Software figure looks anomalously low relative to the sector-level ROIC table below (41.8% for Information Technology) — likely reflects a narrow "Internet Software" sub-sector slice (e.g., unprofitable internet companies) rather than enterprise software broadly; treat with caution and verify directly against Damodaran's published spreadsheet (pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar) rather than this secondhand summary.**
- Sector-level ROIC benchmarks (2026, source aggregation, exact original attribution unclear — treat as approximate): Information Technology 41.8%, Health Care 22.2%, Industrials 20.0%, Consumer Staples 17.1%, Consumer Discretionary 15.8%, Communication Services 14.4%, Materials 13.5%, Energy 12.3%, Utilities 6.5%, Real Estate 5.2% — [stockunlock.com, What Is a Good ROIC?](https://stockunlock.com/investing/what-is-a-good-roic/)
- General ROIC rule of thumb (non-sector-specific): "15% ROIC is worth paying attention to for many mature businesses," "20%+ ROIC is often very strong," though the source cautions against universal cutoffs — [stockunlock.com](https://stockunlock.com/investing/what-is-a-good-roic/)

### Inferences
- **Self-constructed worked example** (built directly from the cited formulas above — ROIC = NOPAT/Invested Capital with NOPAT = EBIT×(1−tax); ROE = Net Income/Equity — to concretely show ROIC staying flat while ROE is inflated by a debt-funded buyback, isolating the capital-structure effect from any operating change):
  - **Year 0 (before buyback):** Revenue $1,000; EBIT $150 (15% EBIT margin); Debt $200 at 5% interest → Interest expense $10; Tax rate 25%.
    - Net Income = (EBIT − Interest) × (1 − tax) = ($150 − $10) × 0.75 = **$105**
    - NOPAT = EBIT × (1 − tax) = $150 × 0.75 = **$112.5**
    - Equity = $800; Invested Capital = Debt + Equity = $200 + $800 = **$1,000**
    - **ROE = $105 / $800 = 13.1%**
    - **ROIC = $112.5 / $1,000 = 11.25%**
  - **Year 5 (after a debt-funded buyback, operations unchanged):** the company borrows an additional $400 and uses 100% of it to repurchase stock. EBIT is unchanged at $150 (no operational improvement — this is the point of the example). Debt rises to $600 at the same 5% rate → interest expense $30. Equity falls by the $400 buyback to $400.
    - Net Income = ($150 − $30) × 0.75 = **$90**
    - NOPAT = $150 × 0.75 = **$112.5** (unchanged — NOPAT is pre-interest, so a financing-only transaction cannot move it)
    - Invested Capital = Debt + Equity = $600 + $400 = **$1,000** (unchanged — total capital is the same, only the debt/equity mix shifted)
    - **ROE = $90 / $400 = 22.5%** (up from 13.1%, a +9.4 percentage-point jump)
    - **ROIC = $112.5 / $1,000 = 11.25%** (completely unchanged)
  - **Conclusion of the example:** ROE rose by ~72% relative (13.1%→22.5%) purely from a financing decision (replacing equity with debt), with zero change in the underlying operating business (EBIT, NOPAT, and total invested capital all identical). ROIC, by construction, cannot be moved by this transaction because it uses pre-interest income (NOPAT) divided by total capital (debt+equity combined), making it capital-structure-neutral — exactly the property that makes ROIC the more reliable "real operational improvement" signal and ROE the more easily gameable one. Net income did decline here (due to the added interest expense), underscoring that reported ROE improvement coexisted with declining absolute profit — a pattern a checklist should specifically screen for (rising ROE + falling or flat net income + rising debt = leverage-driven ROE, not a quality signal).
- Practically, a per-ticker checklist item should compute both ROE and ROIC every year, and separately track Debt/Equity and net buyback dollars (shares repurchased × price); a divergence where ROE is rising/high while ROIC is flat or declining — especially alongside rising leverage or heavy buybacks — should be flagged as a "ROE is leverage/buyback-driven, not operationally driven" warning, consistent with the mechanism described in all three cited numeric examples above.
- Given the conflicting/uncertain sector-level ROIC-WACC figures found (the Internet Software anomaly above), a checklist should treat any single secondhand "sector ROIC vs WACC" figure as indicative only and, where precision matters, go directly to Damodaran's own published spreadsheet rather than aggregator summaries.

### Gaps
- Exact, verified Damodaran sector-by-sector ROIC and WACC figures could not be extracted directly from the PDF fetch attempt (the fetched Data Update PDF returned only binary/encoded content, not readable text); the sector ROIC/WACC numbers above come from secondary aggregation and should be verified against pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar directly (the "roic.xls" / "wacc.xls" datasets) before being treated as precise.
- No single canonical "X percentage point spread = marginal vs. meaningful" academic citation was found beyond the 2-3pp "strong" and 5pp+ "preferred" figures from dividend.school/basisreport — these appear to be practitioner heuristics rather than a peer-reviewed standard, and should be labeled as such in the final report.

## Key Question 4: Debt metrics — Debt/Equity, Net Debt/EBITDA, interest coverage thresholds, and sector variance

### Takeaway
Net Debt/EBITDA below ~1.0x is low leverage, 1.0–3.0x is moderate, and above ~4.0x is high for a typical non-financial corporate — but capital-intensive, regulated sectors (utilities, REITs, telecom) structurally run 3.0–7.0x+ while asset-light software runs near 0x–2.5x (often net cash); interest coverage (EBIT/Interest) above ~4-5x is comfortable for investment-grade issuers while utilities structurally run near 2.4-2.9x due to their stable regulated cash flows; banks and insurers are evaluated on entirely different leverage logic (D/E of 2.0+ is normal/by-design) because their "debt" (deposits/policy liabilities) funds interest-earning assets rather than representing distress risk.

### Cited Findings
- **Net Debt/EBITDA sector ranges**: B2B software/SaaS 0.0–2.5x typical (many net cash), PE-backed software 3–6x; Industrial/manufacturing 1.0–3.0x (cyclical subsectors target the low end through the cycle); Consumer/retail 2.0–4.0x (higher for LBOs); Utilities/infrastructure 3.0–5.5x common given regulated cash flows — [umbrex.com Net Debt-to-EBITDA Ratio](https://umbrex.com/resources/company-analysis/finance/net-debt-to-ebitda-ratio/)
- Additional Net Debt/EBITDA data points: average ratio for S&P 500 companies ≈1.3x; REIT-Office sub-sector averages 8.02x (highest), REIT-Mortgage 7.78x; regulated utilities near 5.2x in late 2025; Technology & Software sector range cited as 0.5x–2.5x; Transportation/communications/utilities sector median 2.32x; manufacturing median 0.38x; general rule cited: below 1.0x = low leverage, 1.0–3.0x = moderate, above 4.0x = high — [search aggregation of FullRatio/Pomegra/FasterCapital industry data, October 2026](https://fullratio.com/net-debt-to-ebitda-by-industry) (note: direct WebFetch of this FullRatio page returned HTTP 403; figures are from the search-engine summary of it, not independently verified against the live page)
- **Interest coverage ratio (EBIT/Interest) by GICS sector** (mean / median, ~500 companies sampled): Information Technology 37.00 / 16.93; Communication Services 32.84 / 9.50; Consumer Discretionary 32.38 / 9.21; Materials 29.83 / 12.11; Health Care 27.32 / 8.49; Industrials 21.48 / 10.43; Consumer Staples 20.59 / 8.89; Financials 19.34 / 7.69; Energy 16.14 / 7.79; Real Estate 16.06 / 3.24; **Utilities 2.87 / 2.40** (lowest by a wide margin); overall sample average 24.26 / 8.41 — [wisesheets.io, Average Interest Coverage Ratio by Industry (2026 Update)](https://blog.wisesheets.io/average-interest-coverage-ratio-by-industry-2026-update/). The source explicitly states: "there is no universal 'safe' threshold; appropriate levels vary significantly by industry."
- **Rating-agency-style coverage bands** (practitioner source, credit-rating framing): investment-grade non-financial corporates generally maintain EBITDA/Interest ≥6–8x and EBIT/Interest ≥3–4x; upper high-yield (BB-rated) issuers run EBITDA/Interest ~4–6x and EBIT/Interest ~2–3x; single-B issuers run EBITDA/Interest ~2–4x with "increased vulnerability to rate and earnings shocks"; for capital-intensive sectors like Utilities and Real Estate, a 3x interest coverage ratio is considered healthy given cash-flow predictability (i.e., a lower absolute bar is acceptable because of stability, not spare capacity) — [search aggregation citing umbrex.com/basisreport.com credit-ratio primers](https://umbrex.com/resources/financial-ratio-primer/leverage-coverage-ratios/)
- **Debt/Equity by sector**: Utilities/telecom typically 1.0–2.0x (regulated utilities range 1.0–2.5x; Electric Utilities industry average 1.62x trailing Q1 2026); Technology companies 0.0–0.5x (software application companies average 0.34x, software infrastructure companies average 0.3x); Banks/insurers "deliberately hold high leverage as part of their core business model," typically D/E ≥2.0x, though this varies by bank type — diversified banks average 1.35x, regional banks average 0.49x — and the source notes that if a bank's leverage were computed the same way as a manufacturer's, it would show 8–12x, underscoring that bank "debt" (mostly customer deposits funding an interest-earning asset book) is not comparable to corporate debt — [search aggregation of FullRatio/csimarket/basisreport, 2026](https://fullratio.com/debt-to-equity-by-industry); [csimarket Electric Utilities Industry Financial Strength](https://csimarket.com/Industry/industry_Financial_Strength_Ratios.php?ind=1201)
- Key framing quote: "A D/E of 2.0 is conservative for a utility but aggressive for a software company, illustrating why industry context is essential when evaluating these ratios." — [search aggregation](https://www.businesssupervisor.com/debt-to-equity-ratio-by-industry/)

### Inferences
- A per-ticker checklist should not use a single fixed Net Debt/EBITDA or interest-coverage cutoff across all tickers. Instead, it should (a) tag each ticker with a sector bucket (software/tech, industrials, consumer/retail, utilities/infra, REIT, financials/banks), (b) apply the sector-specific band from the findings above, and (c) flag as "elevated" only when the company's own ratio is meaningfully above its *sector's* typical range (e.g., >3x Net Debt/EBITDA for a software company is a real flag; >3x for a utility is normal).
- Utilities stand out as the one non-financial sector where very low interest coverage (≈2.4–2.9x) is structurally tolerated by the market because of regulated, highly predictable cash flows — a checklist should not apply the same ≥4-5x comfort threshold used for industrials/tech to utilities.
- Banks require a parallel, separate leverage framework entirely (regulatory capital ratios such as CET1/Tier 1, not D/E or Net Debt/EBITDA) since conventional corporate leverage ratios are not meaningful for deposit-funded institutions — this is a gap to fill with bank-specific capital-adequacy metrics if the checklist needs to cover financials.

### Gaps
- Direct confirmation of FullRatio's live net-debt-to-EBITDA-by-industry table could not be obtained (403 Forbidden on WebFetch); the cited figures are secondhand via search-engine summarization of that page and of related aggregator sites (Pomegra, FasterCapital), not independently verified against primary filings. A production checklist should re-pull this from a licensed data provider (Bloomberg, Capital IQ, or SEC EDGAR cross-sector aggregation) rather than relying on these aggregator figures.
- No specific regulatory capital ratio thresholds (CET1 minimums, Tier 1 leverage ratio) for banks were researched in this pass — flagged above as a gap if the checklist needs full bank coverage.
- Semiconductor/hardware-specific Net Debt/EBITDA and D/E bands (distinct from generic "industrial/manufacturing") were not separately found; semiconductors were bucketed under general industrial/tech ranges in the sources retrieved, but cyclicality may warrant a tighter (lower) comfortable leverage band given earnings volatility — this should be verified with a semiconductor-specific search if higher precision is needed.

## Key Question 5: Free cash flow conversion — formula, healthy benchmarks, and the earnings-quality red flag when net income is positive but FCF is weak/negative

### Takeaway
FCF = Operating Cash Flow − CapEx; FCF margin = FCF/Revenue, with ~10–15% considered a healthy reference point for a mature business and meaningfully higher (15–35%) typical for asset-light software/SaaS; a persistent, growing gap where net income is positive but OCF/FCF is weak or negative is a genuine earnings-quality red flag (aggressive revenue recognition, receivables buildup, or capitalized costs) — but the same pattern is normal and not a red flag for capital-intensive growth businesses (semiconductor fabs, telecom/cloud infrastructure buildouts) that are reinvesting ahead of their current cash-generating capacity.

### Cited Findings
- FCF conversion ratio formula: most often **FCF / EBITDA** (sometimes FCF/EBIT), which "accounts for capital spending" relative to operating profitability — [WallStreetPrep, Free Cash Flow Conversion Rate](https://www.wallstreetprep.com/knowledge/free-cash-flow-conversion-rate-fcf/); [ramp.com, What is cash flow conversion](https://ramp.com/blog/what-is-cash-flow-conversion)
- FCF margin formula: **FCF Margin = Free Cash Flow / Revenue × 100**; "a higher FCF margin indicates more efficient cash generation relative to revenue" — [freecashflow.org, What's a Good FCF Margin?](https://freecashflow.org/blog/free-cash-flow-margin-formula-benchmarks)
- Benchmark levels: "a 10%–15% free cash flow margin is a common reference point for a healthy mature business," while "a high-growth company may intentionally run lower or negative FCF while investing in expansion"; by industry — Software/SaaS 15–35%, Consumer staples 5–15%, Healthcare 8–18%, Manufacturing 2–10%, Energy/utilities 1–8% — [freecashflow.org](https://freecashflow.org/blog/free-cash-flow-margin-formula-benchmarks)
- Earnings-quality red flag framing: "A company with strong net income but weak operating cash flow often signals earnings quality erosion... indicating a business is struggling to sustain its growth with organic cash generation"; "a significant and growing divergence between net income and operating cash flow can be a warning sign, potentially indicating aggressive accounting practices or problems with collecting payments from customers"; "sustained higher net income relative to cash flow from operations can indicate that the company is boosting earnings with accounting entries rather than real economic activity, which can lead to future write-downs" — [search aggregation citing investing.com Cash Flow Quality Guide and stockalarm.io Net Income vs FCF](https://www.investing.com/academy/analysis/cash-flow-quality-guide/)
- Important counterpoint/nuance: "capital-intensive growth industries such as semiconductor fabs, telecom network buildouts, and cloud infrastructure regularly show healthy or growing net income with flat, weak, or negative free cash flow because reinvestment rate exceeds cash the existing business throws off—this is not automatically a red flag"; the most common mechanical cause of the NI/OCF gap is "heavy capital spending or a build-up in working capital that consumes cash even though it does not touch the income statement the same way" — [search aggregation](https://site.financialmodelingprep.com/education/statements/how-to-detect-earnings-quality-erosion-via-cash-flow-statement)

### Inferences
- A per-ticker checklist should compute both the FCF margin (FCF/Revenue) and the FCF conversion ratio (FCF/Net Income, or FCF/EBITDA) as a multi-year trend, and cross-reference the result against the company's CapEx intensity and revenue growth stage: a low/negative FCF margin at a company with high revenue growth and high CapEx (clearly funding future capacity) should be scored differently than the same low/negative FCF margin at a mature, low-growth company with flat CapEx (which is a much stronger red flag).
- Because the question also referenced "FCF/Net Income" explicitly as a conversion ratio, note that sources found predominantly define "FCF conversion" relative to EBITDA, not net income — a checklist can still compute FCF/Net Income as a simpler, complementary "cash realization of earnings" check (values notably and persistently below ~80-100% across several years, especially without a credible growth-CapEx explanation, should be flagged), but should be clear this specific ratio formulation is a derived/common-sense construction rather than one explicitly benchmarked in the sources retrieved.

### Gaps
- No specific numeric benchmark for "FCF/Net Income" (as opposed to FCF/EBITDA or FCF/Revenue) was found in the sources retrieved — this exact ratio formulation and its "healthy" threshold is a gap; the closest analogue found (FCF/EBITDA conversion ratio) does not have a single cited numeric "good" threshold either, only the qualitative description that it "accounts for capital spending."
- No sector-specific FCF-margin benchmark for semiconductors or financials/banks (where "free cash flow" is calculated differently, if at all, given banks' balance-sheet-driven cash flow statements) was found — flagged as a gap for sectors beyond those explicitly listed (software/SaaS, consumer staples, healthcare, manufacturing, energy/utilities).

## Key Question 6: Named frameworks — Greenblatt's Magic Formula, Damodaran's earnings normalization, Piotroski F-Score (9 criteria), Beneish M-Score, and the Sloan accrual ratio

### Takeaway
Four well-documented, named frameworks cover financial statement quality from different angles: Greenblatt's Magic Formula (ROIC + earnings yield ranking, screening for ROIC/ROA ≥25%), Damodaran's earnings-normalization methodology (cleansing non-recurring/financing items and normalizing across full business cycles for cyclicals), the Piotroski F-Score (a 9-point binary checklist across profitability/leverage/efficiency, academically validated by Piotroski 2002), and the Beneish M-Score (an 8-variable regression model for detecting likely earnings manipulators, with a published -1.78 cutoff) plus the related Sloan accrual ratio (a simpler single-ratio accruals-based earnings-quality screen).

### Cited Findings
- **Greenblatt's Magic Formula**: combines two ranked metrics — Return on Capital, calculated as **EBIT ÷ (Net Fixed Assets + Net Working Capital)**, and Earnings Yield, calculated as **EBIT ÷ Enterprise Value** — ranks ~3,500 stocks 1-to-N on each metric (rank 1 = best) and combines the two rankings to find cheap, high-quality businesses; Greenblatt uses EBIT rather than net income specifically "to put companies with different debt levels and tax rates on an equal footing when comparing their returns" — [oldschoolvalue.com](https://www.oldschoolvalue.com/stock-valuation/the-magic-formula-return-on-capital/); for the formal screen, a return on invested capital greater than 25% (or alternatively ROA ≥25%) is used as a quality filter, alongside a stated minimum holding period of 3–5 years and a market-cap floor of roughly $50-100 million — [search aggregation of "The Little Book That Beats the Market" summaries](https://aaii.medium.com/greenblatts-magic-formula-for-beating-the-market-ccfc429287ec)
- **Damodaran's earnings normalization**: involves "cleansing operating items of financial expenses, capital expenses, and non-recurring expenses"; for macro-driven distortions (commodity price swings, recessions), the approach is to normalize earnings and cash flows "across the commodity or economic cycle" rather than trying to forecast the next cycle; a practical shortcut method is to apply the company's **average historical ROE and book value of equity** to derive normalized net income, or similarly apply the **average historical operating/net margin** to current revenue to get normalized operating/net income; critically, Damodaran stresses that return on capital, reinvestment rate, and cost of financing must *all* be normalized together, not just the earnings figure in isolation — [pages.stern.nyu.edu, "More on normalizing earnings"](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/adamodar/New_Home_Page/valquestions/normearn.htm); [pages.stern.nyu.edu, "Ups and Downs: Valuing Cyclical and Commodity Companies"](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/papers/commodity.pdf)
- **Piotroski F-Score — the exact 9 criteria** (1 point each, 0-9 total; source: Joseph D. Piotroski, "Value Investing: The Use of Historical Financial Statement Information to Separate Winners from Losers," University of Chicago GSB, January 2002), organized into three categories per Wikipedia's summary of the methodology:
  - *Profitability (4 points):*
    1. Return on Assets (ROA) is positive in the current year → 1 point
    2. Cash Flow from Operations (CFO) is positive in the current year → 1 point
    3. ROA is higher in the current year than the prior year (ΔROA > 0) → 1 point
    4. CFO/Total Assets is higher than ROA/Total Assets in the current year — i.e., cash flow from operations exceeds net income (an accrual-quality check) → 1 point
  - *Leverage, Liquidity & Source of Funds (3 points):*
    5. Long-term debt-to-total-assets ratio is lower than the prior year (leverage decreased) → 1 point
    6. Current ratio is higher than the prior year (liquidity improved) → 1 point
    7. No new shares were issued in the last year (no dilutive equity offering) → 1 point
  - *Operating Efficiency (2 points):*
    8. Gross margin is higher than the prior year → 1 point
    9. Asset turnover ratio is higher than the prior year → 1 point
  - **Interpretation**: scores of 8-9 are considered strong; scores of 0-2 are considered weak — [Wikipedia, Piotroski F-score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piotroski_F-score)
- **Beneish M-Score — the exact 8 variables and formula** (source: M. Beneish, as summarized on Wikipedia):
  - DSRI (Days Sales in Receivables Index) = (Net Receivables_t / Sales_t) / (Net Receivables_t-1 / Sales_t-1)
  - GMI (Gross Margin Index) = [(Sales_t-1 − COGS_t-1)/Sales_t-1] / [(Sales_t − COGS_t)/Sales_t]
  - AQI (Asset Quality Index) = measures the proportion of assets other than current assets and tangible PP&E (i.e., rising "soft" assets)
  - SGI (Sales Growth Index) = Sales_t / Sales_t-1
  - DEPI (Depreciation Index) = compares depreciation rates across consecutive periods (declining depreciation rate relative to asset base is a flag)
  - SGAI (SG&A Expense Index) = (SG&A_t/Sales_t) / (SG&A_t-1/Sales_t-1)
  - LVGI (Leverage Index) = measures changes in financial leverage between periods
  - TATA (Total Accruals to Total Assets) = (Income from Continuing Operations_t − Cash Flow from Operations_t) / Total Assets_t
  - **Formula**: M-score = −4.84 + 0.92×DSRI + 0.528×GMI + 0.404×AQI + 0.892×SGI + 0.115×DEPI − 0.172×SGAI + 4.679×TATA − 0.327×LVGI — [Wikipedia, Beneish M-score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beneish_M-score)
  - **Threshold**: an M-score above roughly **−1.78** indicates the company is more likely to be an earnings manipulator; below −1.78 is "unlikely to be a manipulator." **Note a sourcing discrepancy**: a separate search-aggregated source cited a slightly different coefficient on TATA (4.697 rather than Wikipedia's 4.679) and referenced an alternate, more conservative threshold of **−2.22** (fewer false positives) alongside the more commonly cited −1.78 — both threshold values appear in circulation; the −1.78 cutoff from Beneish's original published work is the more commonly cited one and should be treated as primary, with −2.22 noted as an alternate/stricter cutoff used by some practitioners — [myaccountingcourse.com / oldschoolvalue.com aggregation](https://www.myaccountingcourse.com/beneish-m-score); [Wikipedia, Beneish M-score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beneish_M-score)
- **Sloan accrual ratio** (Richard Sloan, related earnings-quality/accruals check often cited alongside Beneish and Piotroski): formula given as **Sloan Ratio = (Net Income − Cash From Operations − Cash From Investing) / Total Assets**, with a simplified variant **(Net Income − Operating Cash Flow) / Average Total Assets × 100**; a more granular total-accruals formula is also given as **Accruals = (ΔCurrent Assets − ΔCash) − (ΔCurrent Liabilities − ΔShort-Term Debt − ΔTaxes Payable) − Depreciation**; interpretation: accruals exceeding **+10%** of total assets indicate low earnings quality (profits driven by non-cash adjustments), and empirically "stocks with high accruals have tended to underperform over the following 12 months as accruals revert" — [search aggregation of GuruFocus/stockalarm.io/breakingdownfinance Sloan Ratio explainers](https://www.gurufocus.com/glossary/sloanratio); [breakingdownfinance.com, Sloan Accruals Ratio](https://breakingdownfinance.com/finance-topics/equity-valuation/sloan-accruals-ratio/)

### Inferences
- These four/five frameworks are complementary rather than redundant and map cleanly onto different parts of a per-ticker checklist: Greenblatt's Magic Formula → a quick quality+valuation screen (ROIC level + earnings yield); Damodaran's normalization → an adjustment step applied *before* computing any margin/ROE/ROIC trend for cyclical or one-time-item-affected companies; Piotroski F-Score → a 9-point annual "fundamental improving/deteriorating" scorecard; Beneish M-Score and Sloan ratio → two independent manipulation/earnings-quality tripwires that should both be computed (they use different inputs — Beneish is an 8-variable regression on multiple statement line items, Sloan is a single accruals ratio) and cross-checked against each other; a company flagged by both is a much stronger red flag than one flagged by either alone.
- Given the sourcing discrepancies found (Beneish coefficient 4.679 vs 4.697; threshold −1.78 vs −2.22), a checklist implementation should hardcode the Wikipedia/Beneish-original values (−4.84 intercept, 4.679 TATA coefficient, −1.78 threshold) as the primary calculation, since Wikipedia's version traces directly to the original academic formula, and treat −2.22 only as an optional "stricter" secondary threshold for reducing false positives if desired.

### Gaps
- The original Beneish (1999) paper itself was not directly fetched/read in this pass — the formula and threshold above come from Wikipedia's summary and secondary aggregator sites, not the primary academic paper ("The Detection of Earnings Manipulation," Financial Analysts Journal, 1999); if exact precision on coefficients is critical, the primary paper should be pulled directly.
- The Piotroski (2002) original paper was likewise not directly fetched; the 9-criteria summary above comes from Wikipedia's treatment of it. The exact wording/thresholds should be checked against the original paper ("Value Investing: The Use of Historical Financial Statement Information to Separate Winners from Losers," Journal of Accounting Research, 2002) if the checklist will be published or cited formally.
- No explicit numeric ROA/ROIC threshold distinction was found between Greenblatt's "25% ROIC" screen criterion and his separately-cited "25% ROA" criterion — sources used these somewhat interchangeably/ambiguously, and the precise criterion as published in "The Little Book That Beats the Market" should be verified directly against the book rather than secondary summaries.
