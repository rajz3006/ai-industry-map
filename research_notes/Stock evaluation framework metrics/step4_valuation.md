# Step 4: Valuation — Metrics, Formulas, Thresholds, and Comparison Methods

## Key Question 1: Exact formulas for trailing P/E, forward P/E, PEG, EV/EBITDA, EV/Sales, P/S, P/B, FCF yield — and why absolute thresholds are misleading

### Takeaway
Each multiple has a precise, standard formula, and generic "rule of thumb" cheap/fair/expensive bands exist in practice, but every source that states a band also warns it is only usable after adjusting for sector, growth, margin, capital structure, and the company's own historical trading range — an absolute number in isolation is not interpretable.

### Cited Findings
- Trailing P/E = Price per share ÷ trailing-twelve-month (TTM) EPS; uses actual historical earnings, so it is "more concrete and less subject to forecasting errors" — [Musaffa Academy](https://academy.musaffa.com/how-to-use-trailing-p-e-and-forward-p-e/); [Wikipedia: Price–earnings ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price%E2%80%93earnings_ratio)
- Forward P/E = Current share price ÷ forecasted (analyst-consensus or company-guided) EPS for the next fiscal year — [Wall Street Prep](https://www.wallstreetprep.com/knowledge/forward-pe-ratio/); [SmartAsset](https://smartasset.com/investing/forward-p-e-vs-trailing-p-e)
- Typical "normal" P/E range cited across sources is roughly 15x–30x, though this varies heavily by rate environment and sector — [Stock Rover](https://www.stockrover.com/metrics/valuation/)
- The inverse of P/E is the "earnings yield" (EPS/Price); a P/E of 25 = a 4.0% earnings yield, which can be compared directly against bond yields (e.g., the 10-year Treasury) as a cross-asset cheapness check — [Stock Rover](https://www.stockrover.com/metrics/valuation/)
- EV/EBITDA = Enterprise Value (market cap + total debt − cash & equivalents + minority interest + preferred) ÷ EBITDA; it strips out differences in capital structure, tax rate, and depreciation/amortization policy, which is why it's preferred over P/E for cross-company and cross-border comparisons — [Macabacus](https://macabacus.com/valuation/multiples); [NIFM Academy](https://www.nifmacademy.com/blog/post/429/stock-market/evebitda-explained-why-it-beats-pe-for-comparisons)
- Commonly cited EV/EBITDA bands: under ~8x often called cheap; ~8x–14x typical for established/mature companies; above ~15x implies the market is pricing in high growth — [Stock Rover](https://www.stockrover.com/metrics/valuation/); broader observed range cited as 6x–18x across industries — [Stock Rover](https://www.stockrover.com/metrics/valuation/)
- EV/Sales = Enterprise Value ÷ annual revenue; typically used when a company's EBITDA/earnings are negative or not yet meaningful (e.g., early-stage or loss-making growth companies), since EBITDA- and earnings-based multiples are undefined or distorted in that case — [Wikipedia: EV/Sales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enterprise_value-to-sales_ratio); typical range cited as roughly 1.0x–3.0x for a broad company universe — [Stock Rover](https://www.stockrover.com/metrics/valuation/)
- Price/Sales (P/S) = Market Capitalization ÷ trailing (or forward) total annual revenue — [CFI: Price to Sales Ratio](https://corporatefinanceinstitute.com/resources/knowledge/valuation/price-to-sales-ratio)
- P/S bands cited: below ~1x = potentially undervalued/distressed, common in mature cyclicals, struggling retailers, or commodity businesses (may be a value opportunity OR a sign of fundamental problems); ~1x–3x = moderate, typical of steady-growth established companies (consumer staples, traditional industrials); above ~10x requires "exceptional justification"; above ~20x the market is pricing in "near-perfect execution for many years" — [search synthesis, multiple sources via priceva.com/stockpeg.com/winvesta.in aggregation]
- Reported real-world P/S medians vary enormously by sector: grocery retail ≈0.36x, software ≈11.2x, median public SaaS company ≈5.1x revenue, S&P 500 overall ≈3.45x vs. a longer-run historical mean of ≈1.81x — illustrating why a single cross-sector absolute P/S threshold is meaningless — [aggregated search result, multiple sources]
- Price/Book (P/B) = Share Price ÷ Book Value per Share (shareholders' equity / shares outstanding) — [Capmint: P/B Ratio](https://www.capmint.com/learn/glossary/price-to-book-ratio)
- P/B bands cited: under 1.0x may indicate undervaluation ("deep value"); above 3.0x often considered expensive; a threshold around 2.0x is used by some analysts as a dividing line (below 2.0x = possible value, above = possible overvaluation) — [aggregated: ryanoconnellfinance.com, financehalo.com]
- P/B is especially sector-dependent: cited "normal" ranges include Financials/Banks/Insurance ≈1.5x–2.0x, Energy (Oil & Gas) ≈1.8x–2.5x, Utilities ≈1.8x–2.2x, because these are asset-heavy, balance-sheet-driven businesses where book value is a meaningful proxy for economic value, unlike asset-light software/services businesses — [eqvista: P/B by industry](https://eqvista.com/price-to-book-ratio-by-industry/)
- FCF Yield = Free Cash Flow ÷ Market Capitalization (equity-basis) — OR Free Cash Flow ÷ Enterprise Value (firm-basis, pairing FCFF with EV) — both forms are used in practice and must not be mixed (equity FCF with market cap; firm FCF with EV) — [Quant Investing: FCF Yield](https://www.quant-investing.com/blog/fcf-yield); [Basis Report: FCF Yield Guide](https://www.basisreport.com/resources/free-cash-flow-yield-guide)
- FCF yield bands cited: above ~8% = potentially undervalued (market paying <12.5x annual FCF; historically associated with strong long-run returns for patient holders); above ~5% = attractive; ~2%–5% = moderate; below ~2% = weak/expensive — [aggregated: freecashflow.org, safetymargin.io, onveston.substack.com]
- Rate-environment adjustment rule of thumb: demand an FCF yield that exceeds the 10-year Treasury yield by at least 2–3 percentage points to compensate for equity risk; in a higher-rate (e.g., ~5% T-bond) environment, shift all FCF-yield cutoffs up by roughly 2 points (what was a "cheap" 5% yield becomes merely average; ~7% becomes the new "reasonable" bar) — [onveston.substack.com, via aggregated search]
- General caution echoed across nearly every source: "No single valuation multiple tells you whether a stock is cheap or expensive. Use at least two, compare within sectors, and cross-check before making decisions." — [Stock Rover: Valuation Metrics Explained](https://www.stockrover.com/metrics/valuation/)
- A multiple trading below peers "might be cheap — or it might deserve the discount due to lower growth, worse margins, or higher risk. Always ask why the gap exists." — [Comparable Company Analysis guides, aggregated via search, e.g. ibinterviewquestions.com]

### Inferences
- Absolute thresholds are unreliable for at least four compounding reasons visible across the findings above: (1) sector/business-model differences (software P/S of 11x vs. grocery P/S of 0.36x are each "normal" for their sector); (2) the macro rate environment shifts every yield-based threshold (FCF yield, earnings yield) because they compete with the risk-free rate; (3) capital-structure and accounting differences make EPS- or book-value-based multiples (P/E, P/B) non-comparable across companies with different leverage/depreciation policy, which is exactly why EV/EBITDA and EV/Sales exist as substitutes; (4) a multiple is a single snapshot number that says nothing about whether it's justified by the company's own growth/margin/quality — it must be read against a *reference distribution* (peer set and/or own history), not a universal number line.
- Because EV/Sales and P/S are the multiples of choice precisely for loss-making/high-growth companies, any checklist should branch: use P/E, PEG, FCF yield, P/B as primary for profitable/mature names, and EV/Sales, P/S, Rule-of-40-style growth-adjusted sales multiples for pre-profit growth names.

### Gaps
- Did not find one single authoritative, universally-agreed numeric table of "cheap/fair/expensive" per metric — all cited bands come from financial-education/aggregator sites (Stock Rover, freecashflow.org, eqvista, etc.) rather than a single academic or regulatory primary source. Treat the specific numeric cutoffs above as commonly-repeated industry heuristics, not consensus science. Damodaran's own sector-average datasets (see Key Question 4) are the more rigorous reference-point source for precise current numbers.

## Key Question 2: PEG ratio — origin, the PEG≈1.0 heuristic, and why it breaks down for high-growth/low-earnings companies

### Takeaway
PEG = P/E ÷ expected annual EPS growth rate (as a whole number, not decimal), popularized by Peter Lynch in his 1989 book "One Up on Wall Street"; Lynch's heuristic is that a fairly valued growth company should trade near PEG = 1.0 (P/E roughly equal to its growth rate), with PEG below 1.0 flagged as a potential bargain — but the ratio becomes mathematically unreliable or meaningless once earnings or growth are near zero, negative, or driven by non-operating/one-off items.

### Cited Findings
- PEG Ratio = P/E ratio ÷ expected annual EPS growth rate; popularized by Peter Lynch in his 1989 bestseller "One Up on Wall Street" — [Wikipedia: PEG ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PEG_ratio); [FastGraphs](https://www.fastgraphs.com/blog/the-peg-ratio-peter-lynchs-secret-to-29-annual-returns-and-how-fast-graphs-makes-it-easy-to-use)
- Example of the heuristic: a stock trading at 25x earnings with 25% expected annual growth has PEG = 1.0 (fair value); a company growing 20%/yr "should" trade near a 20x P/E to be at PEG = 1.0 — [aggregated search, FairValuePrice.com / Waver Research]
- Lynch specifically looked for PEG ratios under 1.0 as candidates offering the best return potential (stocks priced cheaply relative to their growth) — [aggregated search]
- PEG can be negative if a company has negative earnings, and a PEG computed off negative present earnings is "viewed with skepticism as almost meaningless, other than as an indication of high investment risk" — [aggregated search; general consensus across stockalarm.io, kotakmf.com, schwab.com style sources]
- PEG explodes toward very large, uninformative values when the growth input is near zero: "2% growth turns any sensible P/E into a PEG of 8 or more," making the ratio "effectively unusable for turnarounds, deep cyclicals at trough earnings, and pre-profit companies with no meaningful E at all" — [aggregated search synthesis]
- A single unusually strong or weak earnings year can distort the growth input (the "G") enough to make the ratio misleading for a year or two afterward — i.e., PEG is highly sensitive to which base year/period is used for the growth calculation — [aggregated search]
- Broader critique: PEG treats all growth as equivalent regardless of how it was funded — growth financed by heavy debt or serial acquisitions is riskier than organically/FCF-funded growth, and PEG does not distinguish between them — [aggregated search]

### Inferences
- PEG is best understood as a *bridge heuristic* between pure earnings-multiple pricing (P/E) and growth — useful specifically in the middle ground of "moderately-growing, solidly profitable" companies (think 10%–30% EPS growth, positive and fairly stable earnings). It is not designed for, and should be explicitly excluded from, (a) pre-profit/negative-earnings companies, (b) deep-value/turnaround names at trough earnings, and (c) hyper-growth companies where growth is so high or so front-loaded that the denominator swings wildly year to year. For those categories, a checklist should substitute EV/Sales-to-growth, Rule of 40, or DCF-based scenario checks instead of PEG.
- Because PEG uses a single-year (or even single-point consensus-estimate) growth figure, a robust checklist implementation should specify *which* growth figure (e.g., next-fiscal-year consensus EPS growth, or a 3–5 year forward CAGR) to reduce the year-to-year distortion noted above.

### Gaps
- Could not verify a precise primary-source quote from "One Up on Wall Street" itself (no direct excerpt was retrieved); the PEG≈1.0 heuristic and Lynch attribution are consistently repeated across secondary/tertiary finance-education sources but a first-party citation from the book's text was not directly fetched in this research pass.

## Key Question 3: Historical-percentile comparison of a stock's own valuation multiple (P/E, EV/EBITDA) vs. its 5-/10-year range

### Takeaway
Practitioners rank a stock's current multiple against the empirical distribution of its own trailing 5- or 10-year multiple history and express it as a percentile; common informal convention flags roughly the bottom quintile (below ~20th percentile) as "cheap relative to own history" and the top quintile (above ~80th percentile) as "expensive relative to own history," with intermediate bands often labeled undervalued/fair/overvalued.

### Cited Findings
- A percentile-rank framework cited in search results categorizes valuation into bands: 0–20 "Attractive," 21–40 "Undervalued," 41–60 "Fair Value," 61–80 "Overvalued," 81–100 "Expensive" — [aggregated, via Trendonify/TradingView-style valuation percentile frameworks]
- A separate cited framework (TradingView-oriented) describes percentile rank of 75–90% as "above-normal valuation," 90–95% as "expensive/stretched," and 95%+ as "extremely expensive" — [TradingView P/E Ratio indicators](https://www.tradingview.com/scripts/price-earnings-ratio/?script_type=indicators)
- Practical illustration: if a stock's current P/E sits at the 89th percentile of its 10-year history, that means the stock's P/E "has almost always been lower than the current level" historically — i.e., it is trading rich versus its own historical norm — [aggregated search synthesis]
- Platforms like Koyfin explicitly offer an "Equity Percentile Rank" / "Percentile Rank Snapshot" feature for comparing a stock's current metric against its own historical distribution — [Koyfin Help: Percentile Rank Snapshot](https://www.koyfin.com/help/percentile-rank-snapshot-feature/)
- As an example of the underlying methodology applied to the broad market: the Shiller/CAPE P/E and P/E10 frameworks (AQR, Advisor Perspectives) are built by comparing a current cyclically-adjusted multiple against the market's own long historical series, the same conceptual method scaled down to a single stock's own 5–10yr history — [AQR: Shiller P/E white paper](https://www.aqr.com/-/media/AQR/Documents/Insights/White-Papers/An-Old-Friend-The-Stock-Markets-Shiller-PE.pdf); [Advisor Perspectives: P/E10 and Market Valuation](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/dshort/updates/2026/06/02/p-e10-and-market-valuation-may-2026)

### Inferences
- The exact percentile cutoffs (20/80 vs. other bands) are not standardized across providers — different platforms draw the "cheap" and "expensive" lines at slightly different points (e.g., quintile-based 20/80 vs. the TradingView-style 75/90/95 staggered bands). For a repeatable checklist, the most defensible and most commonly repeated convention to adopt is: **<20th percentile of own 5–10yr range = "cheap vs. own history"; >80th percentile = "expensive vs. own history"; 20th–80th = "in-line with own history."** This should be explicitly flagged in the checklist as a convention choice, not a universal standard.
- A key methodological caveat practitioners apply (though not always made explicit in these sources): the percentile should be computed on a *consistent* multiple definition (e.g., always trailing GAAP EPS, or always forward consensus EPS) across the lookback window, and the lookback window itself should be long enough to span at least one full business cycle — otherwise a 5-year percentile taken entirely within a single bull market, for instance, will systematically understate what "expensive" really means.
- This own-history percentile method and the peer-comps method (Key Question 4) are complementary, not substitutes — own-history percentile answers "is this expensive versus what *this* market has historically paid for this specific business," while peer comps answers "is this expensive versus what the market currently pays for *similar* businesses." A full checklist should run both.

### Gaps
- Did not find a single canonical/primary-source (e.g., an academic paper or a major data vendor's official methodology page) that defines the 20th/80th percentile convention as a formal standard — it appears to be informal, cross-platform common practice rather than a codified rule. No percentile convention specific to EV/EBITDA (as distinct from P/E) was found separately; the same percentile-of-own-history method is presumed to generalize, but no source explicitly confirmed EV/EBITDA-specific percentile banding.

## Key Question 4: Peer/sector-relative valuation (comparable company analysis / "comps") — standard method and justified vs. unjustified premium/discount

### Takeaway
Standard "comps" methodology: build a peer set of similar-business-model companies (typically 8–15 peers), pull each peer's EV/EBITDA, EV/Revenue, EV/EBIT, and P/E, benchmark against the peer group's median (preferred over the mean because it is more outlier-resistant), and apply that benchmark multiple to the target; any premium or discount the target trades at relative to peers is then explicitly attributed to differences in growth rate, margin/profitability, risk, or market position — a premium/discount is "justified" only insofar as it's explainable by those fundamental differences, and Damodaran's own published cross-sectional regressions of multiples against growth/profitability/risk variables are a primary quantitative tool for testing that.

### Cited Findings
- Comparable Company Analysis ("trading comps") is a relative valuation methodology estimating a target's value by comparing it to publicly traded peers of similar size, industry, and growth profile — [Umbrex: Comparable Company Analysis](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/finance-frameworks/comparable-company-analysis/); [Street of Walls: Comparable Company Analysis](https://www.streetofwalls.com/finance-training-courses/investment-banking-technical-training/comparable-company-analysis/)
- Analysts typically build a peer set of 8–15 companies using platforms like FactSet/Bloomberg, selected on industry classification, business model, size, geography, and growth profile — [aggregated search, via ibinterviewquestions.com style sources]
- Standard sequence: select peer group → spread/compile peer multiples (mainly EV/EBITDA, EV/EBIT, EV/Revenue, P/E) → pick a central-tendency benchmark (commonly the median, since median better screens out outliers than the mean) → apply the benchmark multiple to the target's own financials to derive an implied value — [aggregated, ibinterviewquestions.com / financetutor.app sources]
- "A company trading at 8x EV/EBITDA when peers trade at 12x might be cheap — or it might deserve the discount due to lower growth, worse margins, or higher risk. Always ask why the gap exists." — [aggregated, comps methodology guides]
- EV/EBITDA is the primary multiple for most industrial, consumer, and services companies specifically because it removes the impact of differing capital structures, tax rates, and depreciation policy, making it the most capital-structure-neutral tool for a peer comparison — [aggregated search]
- Growth-adjusted multiple framing: dividing a standard multiple (EV/Revenue or EV/EBITDA) by the company's growth rate produces a "growth-adjusted multiple" showing how much is being paid per percentage point of growth — explicitly described as generalizing the same logic as the PEG ratio and the Rule of 40 to enterprise-value multiples — [Quant Corp Fin: Adjust Valuation Multiple for Growth](https://www.quantcorpfin.com/cookbook/quantitative-analysis/adjust-valuation-multiple-for-growth/)
- Software/SaaS rule of thumb cited: EV/Revenue multiple should be roughly 1/3 of the company's revenue growth rate — [Kellblog: Demystifying the Growth-Adjusted EV/Revenue Multiple](https://www.kellblog.com/demystifying-the-growth-adjusted-enterprise-value-to-revenue-multiple-and-introducing-the-erg-ratio/)
- Profitability/margin affects the justified multiple through the same cash-generation logic as growth: a company with a 30% EBITDA margin will typically trade at a higher EV/Revenue multiple than a 15%-margin peer, because each dollar of revenue converts into roughly twice as much EBITDA — [aggregated search]
- Damodaran publishes actual cross-sectional regressions of valuation multiples (e.g., EV/EBITDA, EV/Sales, P/E) against fundamental drivers (growth rate, reinvestment rate, return on capital, debt ratio, tax rate, risk) across the market, updated periodically, as a quantitative tool to assess whether a company's multiple is "justified" by its fundamentals rather than by eyeballing a peer table — example cited regression form: EV/EBITDA = 15.619 − 0.135×(Debt/Capital) + 0.0116×(Reinvestment Rate) − 0.114×(Tax Rate) + 0.178×g (R² = 7.2%, emerging markets sample) — [Damodaran: Regressions of Multiples](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/MReg05.html)
- Damodaran's NYU Stern site also publishes free, regularly-updated sector-median multiple datasets by GICS-style industry grouping: P/E ratios by sector, EV/EBITDA (Enterprise Value Multiples) by sector, and Price/Sales & EV/Sales (Revenue Multiples) by sector, each downloadable as Excel files — [Damodaran: PE Ratio by Sector (US)](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/pedata.html); [Damodaran: Enterprise Value Multiples by Sector (US)](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/vebitda.html); [Damodaran: Revenue Multiples by Sector (US)](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/psdata.html); [Damodaran: Data Hub (current year)](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datacurrent.html)

### Inferences
- A rigorous per-ticker checklist step for "peer comps" should require not just pulling the peer median multiple, but also explicitly tabulating each peer's (and the target's) growth rate and margin, so any premium/discount vs. the peer median can be attributed: if the target's multiple is above peer median AND its growth/margin are also above peer median, the premium is plausibly justified; if the multiple premium exists without a corresponding growth/margin premium, that is the signal of potential overvaluation (and vice versa for an unjustified discount = potential value opportunity).
- Damodaran's free sector-multiple datasets (pedata.html, vebitda.html, psdata.html) are the single most citable, authoritative, regularly-updated primary source for "what is the current sector-median multiple" — far more rigorous than the generic aggregator-cited bands in Key Question 1 — and should be the anchor data source for the peer-comps step of the checklist rather than generic round-number thresholds.

### Gaps
- Did not directly pull the live numeric values from Damodaran's pedata.html / vebitda.html / psdata.html tables (would require a follow-up fetch of those specific pages/Excel files) — only confirmed their existence, structure, and update cadence (data noted as "last fully updated January 9, 2026" per search result, though this specific date claim should be verified by visiting the page directly before being treated as current). ## Key Question 5: Aswath Damodaran's approach — price vs. value, and DCF basics (FCFF, terminal growth, discount rate) a retail investor could sanity-check

### Takeaway
Damodaran draws a sharp distinction between "pricing" a stock (relative valuation using multiples, benchmarked against comparable assets, driven by market mood/demand-supply) and "valuing" a stock (intrinsic valuation via discounted cash flow, driven by the business's own fundamentals — cash flows, growth, and risk), and argues most market participants — including professional equity research analysts — are actually pricing rather than valuing; his DCF method discounts projected Free Cash Flow to the Firm (FCFF) at the weighted average cost of capital (WACC), with a terminal value computed under the constraint that the perpetual terminal growth rate cannot exceed the long-run nominal growth rate of the economy (roughly the risk-free rate, conceptually ~2–3% real-world developed-market ceiling), and a cost of equity built from CAPM (risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium).

### Cited Findings
- Damodaran: "equity research analysts don't value companies. They price them" — by making the denominators of multiples "as consistent as you can across companies" rather than digging into intrinsic cash-flow fundamentals — [via Motley Fool interview summary](https://www.fool.com/investing/2024/02/10/aswath-damodaran-on-the-difference-between-pricing/)
- Value comes from a company's fundamentals — its capacity to generate and grow cash flows — while price is a market process resulting from supply/demand and market mood plus incremental news flow — [aggregated search, citing Damodaran's "Price and Value: Discerning the Difference" papers]
- Damodaran's reasons most participants default to pricing rather than valuing: (1) data overload pushes people toward simplistic rules/shortcuts rather than deep analysis; (2) pricing via ratio comparison is simply less work than full valuation; (3) widespread statistical misuse (e.g., using means on skewed distributions); (4) ironically, even though data has become far more accessible (reducing the information edge that classic "value investing" shortcuts used to rely on), most practitioners haven't adapted their methodology — [via Motley Fool interview summary](https://www.fool.com/investing/2024/02/10/aswath-damodaran-on-the-difference-between-pricing/)
- If an investor can reasonably estimate intrinsic value and compare it to market price, a market price significantly below estimated value signals a potential buying opportunity — the entire point of doing DCF-based valuation at all rather than only pricing — [aggregated search, citing Damodaran's price-vs-value papers](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/country/valueversuspriceNew.pdf)
- Damodaran's primary source papers on this exact distinction: "Price and Value: Discerning the Difference" (May 2014) and the shorter companion "Price and Value: What's Your Game?" (Feb 2014) — both hosted on his NYU Stern page — [Price and Value: Discerning the Difference (PDF)](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/country/valueversuspriceNew.pdf); [Price and Value: What's Your Game? (PDF)](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/country/valueversuspriceShort.pdf)
- FCFF (Free Cash Flow to the Firm), built from EBIT: FCFF = EBIT × (1 − tax rate) × (1 − Reinvestment Rate) — [Damodaran: The Free Cashflow to Firm Model (PDF)](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/eqnotes/fcff.pdf)
- FCFF, built from Net Income (the more "retail-friendly" starting point since net income is a reported line item): FCFF = Net Income + Depreciation & Amortization + Interest Expense × (1 − Tax Rate) − Capital Expenditures − Net Change in Working Capital — [aggregated search, via Wall Street Prep / standard FCFF derivations]
- Reinvestment Rate = (CapEx − Depreciation + Change in Non-cash Working Capital) ÷ After-tax Operating Income (EBIT×(1-t)) — [aggregated search, Damodaran FCFF framework]
- Fundamental growth-reinvestment identity used in Damodaran's stable-growth/terminal-value stage: Growth rate = Reinvestment Rate × Return on Invested Capital (ROIC); equivalently in stable growth, Reinvestment Rate = stable growth rate ÷ stable-period ROC — worked example cited: 5% growth ÷ 13.61% ROC = 36.73% required reinvestment rate — [aggregated search, Damodaran DCF materials]
- Terminal Value (firm value form) = FCFF_(n+1) ÷ (Cost of Capital − stable growth rate, g_n); Damodaran's constraint: no firm's terminal/perpetual growth rate should exceed the long-run nominal growth rate of the overall economy (for developed markets, roughly the risk-free rate — cited range ~2.0%–3.0% for developed markets) — [aggregated search, Damodaran DCF framework]
- Cost of equity via CAPM = Risk-free rate + Beta × Equity Risk Premium (ERP); for the US, Damodaran's commonly-cited ERP figure used in worked examples is 5.5% (e.g., Cost of Equity = Risk-free rate + Beta × 5.5%); risk-free rate should always be matched to the currency of the cash flows (e.g., 10-year US Treasury yield for USD cash flows, cited developed-market range ~4.2%–4.5%; a local-currency government bond yield, e.g. 10-year G-Sec, for other currencies) — [aggregated search, Damodaran CAPM/cost-of-equity framework]
- WACC (discount rate used to discount FCFF) = cost of equity and cost of debt weighted by their respective market-value proportions in the capital structure — used specifically because FCFF is a pre-debt-service, whole-firm cash flow, so it must be discounted at a rate reflecting the blended claims of both debt and equity holders — [aggregated search, Damodaran WACC framework]
- Damodaran's core DCF/valuation teaching materials are hosted openly on his NYU Stern page, including a "Valuation in 60 minutes" primer and full discounted cash flow lecture notes — [Damodaran: Valuation in 60 Minutes (PDF)](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/adamodar/pdfiles/eqnotes/val1hr.pdf); [Damodaran: Discounted Cash Flow Valuation notes (PDF)](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/eqnotes/packet1a2pg.pdf); [Damodaran: Valuation lecture hub](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/lectures/val.html)

### Inferences
- A retail-investor-usable simplified DCF "sanity check" sequence, synthesized from the above primitives: (1) estimate next 3–5 years of revenue growth and an EBIT or FCF margin to project FCFF (using the Net-Income-based formula, which only needs line items available on any income statement/cash flow statement); (2) discount those projected FCFFs at an estimated WACC (built from CAPM cost of equity + after-tax cost of debt, weighted by market-value capital structure — Damodaran's beta/ERP/risk-free-rate inputs are directly usable here); (3) cap the terminal growth assumption at roughly the long-run GDP growth / risk-free-rate level (~2–3% for developed markets) — never assume perpetual growth above the economy's own long-run growth, which is the single most common DCF modeling error Damodaran warns against; (4) compute terminal value and discount it back; (5) sum discounted explicit-period FCFFs + discounted terminal value = estimated enterprise value, then back out equity value (subtract net debt) and compare the implied per-share intrinsic value to the current market price — if market price is meaningfully below the DCF estimate, that is Damodaran's explicit buy signal logic (and vice versa for a sell/avoid signal).
- Because the terminal value (driven by the terminal-growth-vs-WACC spread) and the discount rate itself are the two most sensitivity-dominant inputs, a checklist implementation should always present the DCF output as a *range* (bull/base/bear case on growth, margin, and discount-rate assumptions) rather than a single point estimate — consistent with how Damodaran teaches sensitivity analysis around these inputs.

### Gaps

## Key Question 6: "Rule of 40" and sector-specific heuristics for high-growth software/SaaS valuation

### Takeaway
The classic Rule of 40 says a SaaS/software company's revenue growth rate (%) plus its profitability margin (%) — most commonly FCF margin or EBITDA margin — should sum to at least 40; Bessemer Venture Partners has more recently proposed a refined "Rule of X" that explicitly weights growth 2–3x more heavily than margin, reflecting that public markets price a point of growth higher than a point of margin.

### Cited Findings
- Rule of 40: a company's annual recurring revenue (ARR) growth rate plus its profit margin should equal or exceed 40%; for private SaaS companies this is commonly computed as ARR growth % + EBITDA margin % — [Drivetrain: Rule of 40 for SaaS Companies](https://www.drivetrain.ai/strategic-finance-glossary/what-is-rule-of-40-saas-companies)
- Bessemer's "Rule of X" (published 2024) reformulates this as: (Growth Rate × Multiplier) + FCF margin, where the multiplier (M) was set at ~2.3x in Bessemer's February 2024 publication, with a general conservative-base range of roughly 2x–3x in a normalized market environment — [Bessemer Venture Partners: The Rule of X](https://www.bvp.com/atlas/the-rule-of-x)
- Bessemer's rationale: a one-point increase in revenue growth creates *compounding* long-run value, while a one-point increase in FCF margin only adds *linear* value, and Bessemer's own data shows public markets empirically pricing growth at roughly 2–3x the weight of margin — [Bessemer: The Rule of X](https://www.bvp.com/atlas/the-rule-of-x); discussion also summarized in [Clouded Judgement: Rule of X](https://cloudedjudgement.substack.com/p/clouded-judgement-22324-rule-of-x)
- Worked example under the 2.3x multiplier: a company growing 25% revenue with a 15% FCF margin scores 2.3×25 + 15 = 72.5 "Rule of X" points, versus a slower company growing 10% with a 30% FCF margin scoring 2.3×10 + 30 = 53 points — illustrating that under this weighting, growth-heavy profiles score meaningfully higher than margin-heavy profiles even at the same raw Rule-of-40 sum (25+15=40 vs 10+30=40, i.e., identical Rule of 40 scores but very different Rule of X scores) — [aggregated search synthesis of Bessemer's Rule of X]
- Separate SaaS-specific growth-adjusted sales-multiple heuristic: EV/Revenue multiple should be roughly 1/3 of the company's revenue growth rate — [Kellblog: Demystifying the Growth-Adjusted EV/Revenue Multiple](https://www.kellblog.com/demystifying-the-growth-adjusted-enterprise-value-to-revenue-multiple-and-introducing-the-erg-ratio/)

### Inferences
- The shift from Rule of 40 to Bessemer's Rule of X reflects the same underlying principle as the PEG ratio and growth-adjusted EV/Sales multiples discussed in Key Questions 2 and 4: the market does not treat a percentage point of growth and a percentage point of margin/profitability as equivalent — growth is systematically priced at a premium because of its compounding effect on future cash flows. A checklist evaluating a high-growth software name for "Step 4: Valuation" should therefore compute both the traditional Rule of 40 score (as a simple pass/fail screen) and ideally the growth-weighted Rule of X score (or the simpler EV/Revenue ÷ growth-rate heuristic) as a secondary, more discriminating check — since two companies can have an identical Rule of 40 score (e.g., 25%+15% vs. 10%+30%) while warranting very different valuations.
- The Rule of 40 (and Rule of X) functions as a *business-quality screen* feeding into, but distinct from, the actual valuation multiple comparison — i.e., it answers "is this company's growth/profitability combination healthy enough to deserve a premium multiple," which should then be cross-checked against where the stock's EV/Sales or EV/EBITDA multiple actually sits versus peers (Key Question 4) and versus its own history (Key Question 3).
