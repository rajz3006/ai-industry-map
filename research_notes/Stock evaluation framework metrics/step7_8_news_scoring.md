## Q1: What is the practical reliability hierarchy of information sources for a public company?

### Takeaway
No single canonical "stock-research reliability hierarchy" exists as a named framework, but synthesizing general source-reliability principles with finance-specific practice yields a consistent, defensible ladder: primary regulatory filings (SEC) and official company statements sit at the top because they carry legal liability for inaccuracy; earnings-call transcripts and investor-day materials are next (company-sourced, less legally binding but still management's own words); sell-side analyst reports are informed secondary analysis with known biases; mainstream financial press is edited secondary reporting; social media/forums are unverified and carry the least structural accountability.

### Cited Findings
- General source-reliability theory ranks sources by "how much structural accountability exists behind them," with sources at the top having passed through verification, editorial oversight, or review by people with professional consequences for being wrong; primary sources (original documents, data, speeches) are closest to the origin of a claim but still require the reader to do interpretation/verification work — companies can manipulate earnings reports just as politicians can deceive in speeches — [Card Catalog hierarchy of sources](https://cardcatalogforlife.substack.com/p/the-hierarchy-of-sources-a-cheat)
- Credible secondary sources include newspapers, magazines, trade journals, and government websites, which sit below primary sources but above unverified/social content — [Card Catalog hierarchy of sources](https://cardcatalogforlife.substack.com/p/the-hierarchy-of-sources-a-cheat)
- A persistent reliability problem with sell-side equity research is analysts' reluctance to issue negative recommendations, with studies showing sell-recommendation rates as low as 0.5% to 14% of total ratings issued — [arXiv: Structure of Financial Equity Research Reports](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.18327)
- Item 2.02 (Results of Operations) and Item 7.01 (Reg FD) 8-K disclosures are "furnished" rather than "filed" with the SEC, which carries a reduced legal-liability standard compared to "filed" items like Item 5.02 — [B2Digital 8-K example / general SEC 8-K reference material](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/725929/000168316822002340/b2d_8k.htm)

### Inferences
- Combining general epistemics (primary > credible secondary > unverified) with SEC-specific legal mechanics (filed vs. furnished, liability exposure) produces a practical hierarchy for a per-ticker checklist: (1) SEC filings (10-K/10-Q/8-K, filed items carry full liability under Exchange Act Section 18) → (2) official company IR materials, earnings-call transcripts, investor-day decks, press releases (company's own words, but 8-K "furnished" items like Item 2.02 have lighter liability) → (3) sell-side analyst reports (informed but structurally biased toward "buy/hold" due to investment-banking relationships) → (4) mainstream financial press (edited, fact-checked, but secondary interpretation) → (5) social media/forums/message boards (lowest accountability, highest noise, fastest but least verified).
- Because Item 2.02 and Item 7.01 are "furnished" rather than "filed," a checklist should treat 8-K items differently by sub-type: Item 1.01, 2.01, 5.02, 1.03, 3.01 (filed, higher liability) vs. Item 2.02, 7.01 (furnished, used for timely results/guidance disclosure but lower liability standard).

### Gaps
- No single named, widely-cited "reliability hierarchy of financial information sources" framework (analogous to the library-science "hierarchy of evidence") was found specific to equity research; the ladder above is inferred/synthesized rather than quoted from one canonical source.
- Could not confirm with a primary source whether Item 7.01 (Reg FD) is explicitly documented as "furnished not filed" — this is standard securities-law knowledge but was not independently verified against a fetched primary source in this research pass.

## Q2: Which SEC 8-K item types matter most for "breaking news" events, and where are earnings call transcripts reliably sourced?

### Takeaway
The highest-signal 8-K items for breaking news are Item 2.02 (earnings results), Item 5.02 (executive/board departures and appointments), Item 1.01 (material definitive agreements), Item 2.01 (completed M&A), Item 1.03 (bankruptcy), Item 3.01 (delisting), and Item 8.01 (catch-all "other events"); companies must file within four business days of the triggering event. Earnings-call transcripts are reliably sourced from company IR pages (official audio/webcast replay) and aggregator sites Seeking Alpha and Motley Fool, with Seeking Alpha offering audio alongside most US transcripts.

### Cited Findings
- Item 2.02 ("Results of Operations and Financial Condition") is triggered when a company publicly announces or releases material, non-public information about results of operations or financial condition for a completed quarterly/annual period; material under this item is "furnished" rather than "filed," which provides a reduced liability standard — [Form 8-K reference material via SEC EDGAR search results](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/725929/000168316822002340/b2d_8k.htm)
- Item 5.02 ("Departure of Directors or Certain Officers; Election of Directors; Appointment of Certain Officers; Compensatory Arrangements of Certain Officers") covers any change in directors or certain officers, including resignation or refusal to stand for reelection due to a disagreement — [SEC Form 8-K / securities law reference](https://www.securitieslawyer101.com/2025/05/27/understanding-form-8-ks/)
- Item 1.01 is titled "Entry into a Material Definitive Agreement"; Item 7.01 is "Regulation FD Disclosure"; Item 8.01 is "Other Events," described as a catch-all for material events not otherwise specified in the form — [Wikipedia: Form 8-K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Form_8-K)
- Additional breaking-news-relevant items: Item 1.02 (Termination of Material Agreements), Item 1.03 (Bankruptcy or Receivership), Item 2.01 (Completion of Acquisition or Disposition of Assets), Item 3.01 (notification of delisting or transfer of listing) — [Wikipedia: Form 8-K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Form_8-K)
- Companies generally have four business days to file Form 8-K after an event triggers the filing requirement — [Wikipedia: Form 8-K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Form_8-K)
- Seeking Alpha allows searching by company name or ticker, selecting "Earnings," and provides audio files alongside many US earnings-call transcripts — [NYU Libraries: Earnings Call Transcripts guide](https://guides.nyu.edu/finance/earnings-call-transcripts); [Koyfin: Top Earnings Call Transcript Platforms](https://www.koyfin.com/blog/top-earnings-call-transcripts-platforms/)
- The Motley Fool provides earnings-call transcripts in each company's "News & Analysis" section (searchable by name/ticker), offering a smaller collection of US transcripts that count toward a free user's monthly article allowance; it is characterized primarily as a stock-recommendation service rather than a research platform — [NYU Libraries: Earnings Call Transcripts guide](https://guides.nyu.edu/finance/earnings-call-transcripts)

### Inferences
- For a per-ticker "breaking news" checklist, the priority 8-K item scan order should be: 2.02 (earnings) → 5.02 (management changes) → 1.01/1.02/2.01 (deals: entry, termination, completion) → 1.03 (bankruptcy) → 3.01 (delisting) → 7.01 (Reg FD guidance/disclosure) → 8.01 (catch-all, often used for press releases on events like restructurings, legal settlements, or guidance updates not covered elsewhere).
- Because both Seeking Alpha and Motley Fool are secondary aggregators (not the primary source), the most authoritative transcript source is the company's own IR website replay/webcast; aggregators should be used as a faster or more easily searchable fallback, cross-checked against the IR page or the 8-K exhibit (companies sometimes furnish transcripts or slide decks as 8-K exhibits under Item 7.01 or 9.01).

## Q3: How do analysts use customers'/suppliers' guidance as a "leading indicator" (cross-reference technique)?

### Takeaway
Hyperscaler capital-expenditure (capex) guidance is cited as the dominant leading demand indicator for semiconductor, server, and storage suppliers because hyperscaler spend vastly exceeds the chip-equipment industry's own capex, giving early visibility into order books; separately, the semiconductor-equipment industry's book-to-bill ratio (orders received ÷ amount billed) is a standard, named leading indicator for the broader chip-equipment cycle.

### Cited Findings
- The four major US hyperscalers (Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta) purchased $433.9B of property and equipment in the four quarters through March 2026, roughly 4x the combined ~$110B annual capex of TSMC, Samsung, Micron, and SK Hynix combined — this scale differential is cited as making hyperscaler capex "the single best forward indicator of demand for advanced-node wafer capacity" — [siliconanalysts.com: Hyperscaler AI Capex by Company](https://siliconanalysts.com/market-data/hyperscaler-ai-capex)
- Combined CY2026 capex outlook across the four hyperscalers is $720–745B, with some analysts (Morgan Stanley) projecting ~$800B in 2026 and $1.2 trillion in 2027; BofA highlighted nine specific semiconductor stocks (including Intel and Broadcom) as beneficiaries tied to this hyperscaler capex outlook — [Benzinga: Hyperscaler Capex Seen At $1.2T](https://www.benzinga.com/markets/tech/26/08/60886732/hyperscaler-capex-1-2-trillion-9-semiconductor-stocks-bofa)
- Named beneficiary segments of rising hyperscaler capex include GPU suppliers (NVIDIA), memory vendors (SK Hynix, Samsung, Micron), data-center infrastructure providers, and semiconductor-capital-equipment makers (KLA, Applied Materials) that sell the tools needed to manufacture increasingly complex chips — [siliconanalysts.com: Hyperscaler AI Capex Depreciation Wall](https://siliconanalysts.com/analysis/hyperscaler-ai-capex-depreciation-wall-2026)
- The book-to-bill ratio (also called BB ratio or BO/BI ratio) is the ratio of orders received to amount billed over a period (usually one month or quarter); a ratio above 1.0 means more orders were received than filled (strong demand signal), below 1.0 means weak demand — [Wikipedia: Book-to-bill ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book-to-bill_ratio)
- SEMI (Semiconductor Equipment and Materials International) publishes a monthly book-to-bill report for semiconductor-manufacturing-equipment (SME) makers headquartered in North America, released as a three-month rolling average approximately three weeks after each month closes; this data is "closely watched within the industry and by semiconductor market analysts and investors" as an important leading indicator of demand trends — [Wikipedia: Book-to-bill ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book-to-bill_ratio); [Cimetrix: Semiconductor Equipment Book-to-Bill](https://www.cimetrix.com/blog/bid/113583/semiconductor-equipment-industry-posts-book-to-bill-ratio-of-0-94)

### Inferences
- The cross-reference technique generalizes as: a downstream buyer's disclosed/guided capex or order intentions (the "demand signal") is tracked by analysts covering upstream suppliers, because the buyer's spending commitments precede the supplier's revenue recognition by one or more quarters — the same logic applies beyond semis (e.g., airline capex guidance as a leading indicator for Boeing/Airbus suppliers, auto OEM production schedules as a leading indicator for parts suppliers), though this research focused specifically on the hyperscaler-capex-to-chip-supplier and book-to-bill examples named in the brief.
- For a per-ticker checklist, this suggests adding an explicit "check the 2-3 largest customers' most recent capex/guidance commentary" step alongside the company's own guidance, since customer guidance often moves supplier stocks before the supplier's own earnings report.

### Gaps
- Did not independently verify the specific numeric magnitude/direction of the correlation between book-to-bill readings and subsequent equipment-maker stock or revenue performance (i.e., no backtested statistic was found quantifying the leading-indicator lag or predictive strength).

## Q4: What is "expectations investing," and who writes about it?

### Takeaway
"Expectations investing," a framework developed by Michael J. Mauboussin and Alfred Rappaport in their book *Expectations Investing: Reading Stock Prices for Better Returns*, reverses the standard valuation approach: instead of forecasting cash flows and comparing to price, it starts from the current stock price, reverse-engineers the market's implied expectations for sales/margins/investment efficiency, and asks whether those embedded expectations are too high or too low — meaning news should be judged against what the price already assumes (consensus/whisper expectations), not against an absolute "good or bad" standard. Empirically, the related "whisper number" research shows stocks react more strongly to beats/misses versus unofficial whisper expectations than versus official consensus estimates, supporting the expectations-investing premise.

### Cited Findings
- Expectations investing reverses the traditional valuation approach: "Most investors make an estimate of value and then compare it to the current market price. Expectations investing starts with what we know, the stock price, and asks whether the expectations for the company's financial performance implied by the stock price are justified." — [Shortform: Expectations Investing summary](https://www.shortform.com/summary/expectations-investing-summary-michael-j-mauboussin-and-alfred-rappaport)
- The book is authored by Michael J. Mauboussin and Alfred Rappaport, introducing an "expectations infrastructure" framework and "value catalysts" (volume, price/mix, operating leverage, economies of scale, cost efficiency, investment efficiency) as the fundamental forces that trigger revisions in market expectations — [Shortform: Expectations Investing summary](https://www.shortform.com/summary/expectations-investing-summary-michael-j-mauboussin-and-alfred-rappaport)
- A revised/updated edition discussion between Mauboussin and Rappaport is published via The Motley Fool — [The Motley Fool: Expectations Investing Q&A with Mauboussin and Rappaport](https://www.fool.com/investing/2022/01/19/expectations-investing-qanda-mauboussin-rappaport/)
- A 2002 research report found stocks beating the "whisper number" (an unofficial, crowd-sourced earnings expectation) gained an average of more than 2% on the day, versus ~0.1% average gain for stocks that beat official consensus but missed the whisper number — directly illustrating that market reaction tracks the gap versus embedded/informal expectations, not the absolute result — [Wikipedia: Whisper number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whisper_number)
- Bloomberg News found whisper numbers missed actual reported earnings by an average of 21%, versus 44% average miss for officially published consensus estimates — whisper numbers are more accurate predictors, which the source ties to why markets react more strongly to them — [Wikipedia: Whisper number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whisper_number)
- Companies exceeding the Earnings Whisper number saw an average five-day gain of 0.6% from the opening price post-announcement; companies falling short saw an average five-day decline of the same magnitude — [Earnings Whispers: Post-Earnings Announcement Drift](https://www.earningswhispers.com/about-pead)

### Inferences
- For a per-ticker checklist, "expectations investing" operationalizes as: before reacting to any news item, explicitly identify (a) the official consensus estimate/guidance, (b) any available whisper number or informal "buy-side whisper," and (c) what the stock's current valuation multiple implies about expected forward growth/margins — then classify the news as a "beat/miss versus consensus" AND separately as a "beat/miss versus embedded/whisper expectations," since the latter is more predictive of near-term price reaction per the whisper-number research above.

### Gaps
- Did not fetch Mauboussin/Rappaport's original book text directly (publisher page and summaries only), so some mechanical details of the "expectations infrastructure" (e.g., the exact multi-step reverse-DCF process) are summarized at a conceptual level rather than reproduced with full step-by-step formulas.

## Q5: What are the exact mechanics of Joel Greenblatt's "Magic Formula"?

### Takeaway
The Magic Formula ranks every stock in a universe twice — once by earnings yield (EBIT/Enterprise Value) from highest to lowest, and once by return on capital (EBIT/(Net Working Capital + Net Fixed Assets)) from highest to lowest — then sums each stock's two rank numbers into a single combined score; stocks with the lowest combined rank sum (i.e., ranked well on both dimensions) are the top picks, typically held for one year and then replaced.

### Cited Findings
- Earnings Yield = EBIT / Enterprise Value (measures cheapness: operating income per dollar of total company cost, i.e., equity + debt) — [Stablebread: Magic Formula Investing](https://stablebread.com/magic-formula-investing/)
- Return on Capital = EBIT / (Net Fixed Assets + Net Working Capital) (measures quality: how efficiently the business converts invested capital into operating earnings) — [Stablebread: Magic Formula Investing](https://stablebread.com/magic-formula-investing/)
- Ranking mechanics: "Rank them from 1 to N on earnings yield (highest yield gets rank 1). Rank them from 1 to N on return on capital (highest return gets rank 1). Add the two ranks for each stock. The stocks with the lowest combined rank are the Magic Formula picks." — [Stablebread: Magic Formula Investing](https://stablebread.com/magic-formula-investing/)
- Example given: "a stock that ranks 10th on EY and 99th on ROIC gets a value of 109. The two ranks are simply added together and all stocks are ranked on the sum of the two ranks. The stocks with the lowest values are best." — [quant-investing.com: Magic Formula backtest](https://www.quant-investing.com/blog/magic-formula-investment-strategy-back-test)
- Strategy philosophy: look for companies with high earnings yield (undervalued) and high return on invested capital (quality); stocks ranking well on both dimensions are bought and held for approximately one year, then replaced — [Stablebread: Magic Formula Investing](https://stablebread.com/magic-formula-investing/); [Acquirer's Multiple: How to Beat the Market with Greenblatt's Simple Strategy](https://acquirersmultiple.com/2025/07/how-to-beat-the-market-with-greenblatts-simple-strategy/)

### Inferences
- The combined-rank-sum mechanic is directly reusable as a template for any two-factor (or extendable to multi-factor) composite ranking system: rank each factor independently across the comparison universe (1 = best), sum the per-stock rank numbers across factors, then sort ascending by the summed rank — lower sum = better composite rank. This is the same mechanical pattern referenced for Step 8's weighted-rubric and other composite systems.

### Gaps
- Did not independently verify Greenblatt's original source text (*The Little Book That Beats the Market*) directly — mechanics are drawn from secondary explainer sites (Stablebread, quant-investing.com, Acquirer's Multiple) that consistently agree with each other, which increases confidence, but the primary book was not fetched in this research pass.
- Exact enterprise value formula variant (e.g., treatment of minority interest, preferred stock, excess cash) used in Greenblatt's original screen was not confirmed in detail.

## Q6: What other well-known multi-factor composite scoring systems exist (Piotroski F-Score, O'Shaughnessy Trending Value)?

### Takeaway
The Piotroski F-Score is a 0–9 point additive composite built from nine binary (1-or-0) accounting tests spanning profitability, leverage/liquidity, and operating efficiency, with higher scores indicating stronger financial position. O'Shaughnessy's "Trending Value" composite (from *What Works on Wall Street*) combines a multi-factor value composite (Value Composite 2, built from 5–6 valuation ratios) with a momentum overlay — taking the top decile on value, then selecting the top 25 stocks from that group by 6-/12-month price momentum.

### Cited Findings
- The Piotroski F-Score is a number between 0 and 9 assessing financial-position strength, named after Stanford accounting professor Joseph Piotroski, using nine criteria across three categories: profitability, leverage/liquidity, and operating efficiency, each criterion worth exactly one point — [Wikipedia: Piotroski F-score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piotroski_F-score)
- Profitability criteria include: ROA > 0 in the current year (1 point), positive operating cash flow (CFO) in the current year (1 point), among others (change in ROA, and whether CFO exceeds net income/accruals) — [MarketXLS: Piotroski Score guide](https://marketxls.com/blog/what-is-piotroski-score-and-what-is-its-role-in-value-investing)
- Leverage/liquidity criteria include: lower long-term-debt-to-total-assets ratio versus prior year (1 point), higher current ratio versus prior year (1 point), and no new shares issued during the year (1 point) — [MarketXLS: Piotroski Score guide](https://marketxls.com/blog/what-is-piotroski-score-and-what-is-its-role-in-value-investing)
- Operating-efficiency criteria include: improving gross margin versus prior year (1 point) and improving asset-turnover ratio versus prior year (1 point) — [MarketXLS: Piotroski Score guide](https://marketxls.com/blog/what-is-piotroski-score-and-what-is-its-role-in-value-investing)
- Each of the nine criteria is scored 1 (pass/good) or 0 (fail/bad); the F-Score is the simple sum of the nine binary scores, ranging 0–9, with higher indicating stronger financial position — [Wikipedia: Piotroski F-score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piotroski_F-score)
- O'Shaughnessy's Trending Value is described as a composite six-factor strategy claiming a 21.3% CAGR over 40+ years in his original backtest; it takes the top decile of "Value Composite 2" (VC2) — itself built from 5–6 valuation ratios rather than a single metric — and then selects the top 25 stocks from that decile based on 12-month price momentum — [quant-investing.com: O'Shaughnessy Trending Value](https://www.quant-investing.com/strategies/oshaughnessy-trending-value-investment-strategy); [Estoppey Value Investments: Trending Value Strategy](https://valueinvestments.ch/en/lexikon/trending-value-strategy/)
- O'Shaughnessy described Trending Value in the fourth edition of *What Works on Wall Street* as "the best stock market strategy of the last 50 years," with the original 45-year backtest (1964–2009) beating the market by roughly 10% per year — [whatworksintrading Substack: 24 Years Later replication](https://whatworksintrading.substack.com/p/what-works-on-wall-street-24-years)
- A more recent out-of-sample replication found a -10.2% annualized performance gap versus the historical backtest, an "enormous divergence in terminal wealth when compounded over 25 years," indicating the strategy underperformed its historical benchmark in the post-2009 out-of-sample period — [whatworksintrading Substack: 24 Years Later replication](https://whatworksintrading.substack.com/p/what-works-on-wall-street-24-years)

### Inferences
- Both systems follow the same general composite-construction pattern as Greenblatt's Magic Formula: reduce several raw metrics to normalized scores (ranks, percentiles, or binary pass/fail flags), then combine via simple addition (Piotroski, Magic Formula) or a sequential filter-then-rank cascade (O'Shaughnessy: filter to top value decile, then rank survivors by momentum) — useful as two distinct composite-construction templates (additive-score vs. filter-and-overlay) for a checklist builder to choose between.
- The Trending Value out-of-sample underperformance is a useful caution for a composite-scoring checklist: historical backtest CAGR figures for named multi-factor strategies should be treated as upper-bound/best-case rather than forward expectations.

### Gaps
- Did not confirm the exact five or six individual ratios that comprise O'Shaughnessy's "Value Composite 2" (VC2) from a primary source in this pass (commonly cited as combining P/E, P/B, P/S, P/CF, EV/EBITDA, and shareholder yield, but this specific list was not independently verified against a fetched primary source here).
- Did not locate the specific numeric F-Score threshold bands commonly used in practice (e.g., "8–9 = strong, 0–2 = weak") from a primary methodology source; Piotroski's original paper classifies high scores (typically 8–9) as strong and low scores (0–2) as weak in general practitioner usage, but this threshold convention was not independently confirmed via a fetched source in this research pass.

## Q7: How do practitioners translate price targets into a risk/reward ratio, and what minimum ratio is commonly cited?

### Takeaway
The standard formula is Reward = Target Price − Entry Price, Risk = Entry Price − Stop/Downside Price, and Risk/Reward Ratio = Reward ÷ Risk; practitioners commonly cite a minimum acceptable ratio of 2:1, with 3:1 regarded as solid risk management, and these ratios are explicitly tied to the win rate needed to break even or stay profitable.

### Cited Findings
- "Most professional traders aim for a minimum of 1:2, meaning the potential reward is at least twice the risk. A ratio of 1:2 is generally considered the minimum acceptable for most traders, while 1:3 is widely regarded as solid risk management practice." — [Tradezella: Risk-Reward Ratio guide](https://www.tradezella.com/blog/risk-reward-ratio)
- "With a 2:1 ratio, you only need to win 34 percent of your trades to break even. In contrast, at 1:3 you only need 25% win rate to be profitable." — [Tradezella: Risk-Reward Ratio guide](https://www.tradezella.com/blog/risk-reward-ratio)
- "Most day traders should target 2:1 to 3:1 ratios. At these ratios, a 40 to 50 percent win rate is profitable." — [Tradezella: Risk-Reward Ratio guide](https://www.tradezella.com/blog/risk-reward-ratio)
- The risk-reward ratio is described as "your plan before the trade, representing planned reward divided by planned risk set before entry," and the ratio should match the technical structure of the trade rather than be chosen to make the math look favorable after the fact — [Tradezella: Risk-Reward Ratio guide](https://www.tradezella.com/blog/risk-reward-ratio)

### Inferences
- Applying Van Tharp's expectancy math (see Q8) to these ratios: a 2:1 reward/risk trade needs roughly >33% win rate to be net-expectancy-positive before costs, and a 3:1 trade needs roughly >25% — consistent with the Tradezella breakeven figures above, cross-validating the standard reward/risk formula against the R-multiple expectancy framework.
- For a per-ticker checklist, Step 8 should compute Risk/Reward explicitly as (Target Price − Entry Price) / (Entry Price − Stop Price) and gate position entry/sizing on a minimum threshold (2:1 commonly cited as the floor, 3:1 preferred), consistent across both the "price-target" framing (investing context) and the "stop/entry" framing (trading context) described in the sources.

### Gaps
- Sources describing the 2:1/3:1 thresholds are trading-education sites (Tradezella and similar) rather than an academic or named-investor primary source; no single famous investor (e.g., analogous to Mauboussin for expectations investing) was found explicitly credited as the originator of the "2:1 minimum" convention — it appears to be convention/folklore across the trading-education industry rather than attributable to one named framework.

## Q8: How do R-multiples (Van Tharp) relate position sizing to a multiple of initial risk?

### Takeaway
Van Tharp defines "R" (R-value) as the initial per-share or per-trade risk, set by the planned stop-loss distance from entry (R = Entry Price − Stop Price), and "R-multiple" as any trade's profit or loss expressed as a multiple of that initial risk (e.g., a trade that nets twice the dollar risk is a +2R trade); system "expectancy" is the probability-weighted average R-multiple across all trades, and Tharp's framework explicitly shows that a system can be profitable with a low win rate if winning R-multiples are large enough.

### Cited Findings
- "R-value — The initial risk taken in a given position, as defined by one's initial stop loss. R-multiple — All profits can be expressed as a multiple of the initial risk (R). For example, a 10-R multiple is a profit that is 10 times the initial risk. Thus, if your initial risk is $10, then a $100 profit would be a 10 R-multiple profit." — [Van Tharp Institute: A Short Lesson on R and R-Multiples (PDF)](https://vantharp.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/A_Short_Lesson_on_R_and_R-multiple.pdf)
- Worked example: "if you buy a stock at $50 and you plan to get out if it drops to $47 or below, then your R-value per share in this trade is $3.00 (i.e., $50 - $47 = $3.00). If you buy 100 shares of stock your total risk for the trade is $300 — which is your total 1R value." If exited at $47, that's a −1R trade (lost exactly what was risked); if exited at $56, that's a +2R trade ($56 − $50 = $6, $6 ÷ $3 = 2) — [Van Tharp Institute: A Short Lesson on R and R-Multiples (PDF)](https://vantharp.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/A_Short_Lesson_on_R_and_R-multiple.pdf)
- Losses can exceed −1R when the market gaps through the stop price or when psychological mistakes prevent exiting at the planned stop point; excessive costs (commissions, slippage) can also push losses beyond −1R — [Van Tharp Institute: A Short Lesson on R and R-Multiples (PDF)](https://vantharp.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/A_Short_Lesson_on_R_and_R-multiple.pdf)
- Expectancy is defined as "a mathematical formula that tells you how much you will win on the average per dollar risked," calculated by taking the probability-weighted average of all R-multiples (positive and negative) across a system's trades — it represents the system's average R-multiple ("mean R") per trade — [Van Tharp Institute: A Short Lesson on R and R-Multiples (PDF)](https://vantharp.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/A_Short_Lesson_on_R_and_R-multiple.pdf)
- Worked expectancy example: a system right 30% of the time with winners at +15R (30% move after $1 initial risk per share) and losers at −1R the other 70% of the time: expectancy = (0.3 × 15) + (0.7 × −1) = 4.5 − 0.7 = 3.8R average gain per trade, despite being "right" only 30% of the time — illustrating that expectancy (not win-rate/probability) determines long-run profitability — [Van Tharp Institute: A Short Lesson on R and R-Multiples (PDF)](https://vantharp.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/A_Short_Lesson_on_R_and_R-multiple.pdf)
- Van Tharp's core position-sizing question for every trade is reduced to one question: "How much do I risk on each position?" — established through the initial stop price (risk per share) and the number of shares purchased (total dollar risk) — [Van Tharp Institute: A Short Lesson on R and R-Multiples (PDF)](https://vantharp.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/A_Short_Lesson_on_R_and_R-multiple.pdf)
- R-multiples popularized by Van K. Tharp in his book *Trade Your Way to Financial Freedom*; the underlying "normalize by risk" concept predates Tharp but he codified and popularized it for retail traders — [Trademetria: What Are R-Multiples](https://trademetria.com/blog/what-are-r-multiples-the-key-metric-every-trader-should-know/)

### Inferences
- R-multiples directly operationalize position sizing for Step 8: once Risk ($ per share, or % of entry price) is defined by the stop-loss distance, the dollar amount risked per trade (position size × R) can be set as a fixed percentage of account equity (Tharp's materials reference risking "1% of total equity" as a worked example), making the R-multiple framework compatible with — and numerically equivalent to — the Risk/Reward ratio concept in Q7 (Risk/Reward ratio = the "planned R-multiple" of the trade before entry; e.g., a planned 3:1 reward/risk trade is a "planned +3R" trade).
- For a checklist, this suggests logging every closed position's realized R-multiple (not just $ or % P&L) to allow computing system-level expectancy over time, consistent with Tharp's explicit distinction between "probability" (win rate) and "expectancy" (probability-weighted average R) as two separate, independently trackable metrics.

### Gaps
- The source fetched (Van Tharp Institute's short lesson) is promotional material for a paid "Position Sizing Profit Trainer" tool; while the R/R-multiple/expectancy definitions and worked examples are consistent with the standard R-multiple literature cited elsewhere (Trademetria, crosstrade.io), the specific numeric trading-system examples (e.g., "60% win rate, 55% at 1R, 5% at 10R") describe a hypothetical training-game scenario, not an empirical real-market statistic, and should not be cited as real market base rates.

## Q9: How do simple weighted scoring rubrics work, and what score bands are typically used to bucket decisions?

### Takeaway
Practitioner composite-scoring systems generally normalize several factors to a common 0–100 (or similar) scale, apply explicit category weights that sum to 100%, and sum the weighted sub-scores into one composite; decision bands then bucket the composite score into tiers (e.g., Stockopedia's "Super Stock" ≥60 on all three of Quality/Value/Momentum vs. "Sucker Stock" <40 on all three, or a "90/70" buy-above/review-below guideline).
