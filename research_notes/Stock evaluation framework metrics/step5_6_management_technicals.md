# Step 5 (Management & Capital Allocation) and Step 6 (Chart/Technical Read) — Reference for a Per-Ticker Checklist

## Key Question 1: How do investors track insider buying/selling — what form, where sourced, and what patterns are meaningful signals (cluster buying, purchase size vs. holdings, open-market buys vs. 10b5-1 sales)?

### Takeaway
Insider transactions are legally required to be reported on **SEC Form 4** within **two business days** of the transaction, filed on SEC EDGAR and mirrored by free aggregators like openinsider.com; the highest-signal pattern is **cluster buying** (3+ distinct insiders making open-market purchases within a short window, commonly ~2 weeks to 60 days depending on the aggregator), while routine sales executed under **Rule 10b5-1** pre-arranged plans carry much less informational value than discretionary open-market trades.

### Cited Findings
- Form 4 must be filed by officers, directors, and >10% beneficial owners, reporting any change in beneficial ownership (common stock and derivative securities such as options/warrants/convertibles), and must be filed within two business days of the transaction (by 10 p.m. ET on that second business day, keyed to trade date not settlement date) — [WallStreetMojo: SEC Form 4](https://www.wallstreetmojo.com/sec-form-4/); [SEC EDGAR Full Text Search guidance](https://apify.com/jungle_synthesizer/sec-insider-trading-crawler)
- Form 4 transaction codes: **P** = open-market/private purchase, **S** = open-market/private sale, **A** = grant/award (non-market acquisition), **M** = exercise/conversion of a derivative security, **G** = gift, **F** = shares withheld/disposed to pay tax liability on vesting or option exercise; a catch-all **J** code covers transactions not otherwise classified — [WallStreetMojo: SEC Form 4](https://www.wallstreetmojo.com/sec-form-4/)
- SEC EDGAR's full-text/structured search lets users filter by form type (3, 4, 5), company, CIK, date range, etc., and is the primary/official source for Form 4 data — [SEC: Insider Transactions and Forms 3, 4, 5](https://www.sec.gov/files/forms-3-4-5.pdf) (fetch of this PDF returned corrupted binary content in this research pass, so details beyond the search-result summary could not be independently re-verified; see Gaps)
- openinsider.com defines a **cluster buy** as 3 or more distinct corporate insiders (officers, directors, or 10% owners) independently purchasing shares in the open market within a short window, using a reported ~60-day rolling window, and counts only open-market purchase transactions (code P) — excluding awards, gifts, tax-withholding dispositions, and option exercises, because those don't reflect a discretionary capital decision — [OpenInsider Latest Cluster Buys](http://openinsider.com/latest-cluster-buys); [Glama MCP: insider_cluster_buys tool description](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/TradestarV5/insider-signals/tools/insider_cluster_buys)
- Academic research (Lakonishok & Lee, 2001, as cited by secondary sources) found cluster buying produced stronger future returns than individual insider purchases, and that "opportunistic" purchases (the type cluster screens flag) showed materially stronger predictive power — one secondary source cites roughly 5.2% 6-month alpha above benchmark for opportunistic cluster buys; another secondary source states clusters of 3+ insiders buying within 15 days are linked to above-market 12-month returns, and that cluster buys "historically generate roughly double the excess return of single buys" — [MarketTriage summary citing Lakonishok & Lee (2001)](https://markettriage.com/insider-trading-signals); [OpenInsider methodology summary](http://openinsider.com/latest-cluster-buys) — note: these are secondary/aggregator summaries of the academic claim, not a direct citation of the original 2001 paper, so the exact figures should be treated as approximate until the primary paper is checked.
- Rule 10b5-1 (adopted by the SEC in 2000) gives insiders an affirmative defense against insider-trading liability by pre-committing to a trading schedule set in advance, before any material non-public information is known at execution time — [Form4API: SEC Rule 10b5-1 Plans Explained](https://www.form4api.com/guides/10b5-1-trading-plans)
- Academic/practitioner consensus: "Automated 10b5-1 trades tend to have much less informational value than standard open-market trades" because the decision was locked in well before the execution date, disconnecting it from the insider's current view of the stock; this applies overwhelmingly to **sales** — 10b5-1 plans are used far more often for sales than purchases, and planned purchases under 10b5-1 are comparatively rare and typically smaller, because purchases don't carry the same insider-trading legal risk that prompts insiders to use the plan in the first place — [Form4API: 10b5-1 Plans](https://www.form4api.com/guides/10b5-1-trading-plans); [ScienceDirect: When and how are rule 10b5-1 plans used for insider stock sales?](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X23000697)
- By contrast, **discretionary open-market purchases**, especially after a stock has fallen, are read as more bullish because they represent a decision made at a specific moment reflecting the insider's then-current view — [Form4API: 10b5-1 Plans](https://www.form4api.com/guides/10b5-1-trading-plans)

### Inferences
- A repeatable checklist item should be: (1) pull Form 4 filings for the ticker from SEC EDGAR or openinsider.com for the trailing 3–6 months; (2) flag any 3+ distinct insiders with open-market Code-P purchases within a ~2–8 week window (cluster buy); (3) separately flag Code-S sales and check the filing/Form 4 footnotes for "Rule 10b5-1" language — treat 10b5-1 sales as low-signal "noise," and discretionary (non-10b5-1) sales, especially by multiple insiders or the CEO/CFO, as a stronger negative signal; (4) size the purchase relative to the insider's existing stake/annual compensation (a $50K buy by a CEO with no other holdings is a bigger signal than a $50K buy by a director who already holds $20M of stock) — the research surfaced the qualitative principle (compare purchase size to existing holdings and to it being a real "capital decision") but no specific numeric threshold (e.g., "buy must be >X% of base salary") was found in sourced material.
- Because the SEC PDF itself could not be parsed, treat exact transaction-code definitions as corroborated by secondary sources (WallStreetMojo) rather than a primary-source re-read in this pass.

- No specific numeric rule (e.g., "purchase must exceed X% of insider's annual compensation" or "X% of existing holdings") for sizing purchase-relative-to-holdings was found in sourced material; this remains a qualitative heuristic only.

## Key Question 2: How do analysts evaluate capital allocation track record across dividends, buybacks, debt paydown, M&A, and reinvestment — what's a sensible framework for judging good vs. bad allocation?

### Takeaway
The standard framework (most associated with Michael Mauboussin) treats capital allocation as management's most important job, judged by comparing **Return on Invested Capital (ROIC) to the cost of capital (WACC)** for reinvestment decisions, by whether **buybacks were executed when the stock traded below intrinsic value** (value-accretive) versus above it (value-destructive, benefiting sellers at the expense of remaining holders), and by the **frequency/size of goodwill impairments** as the concrete, checkable record of M&A discipline.

### Cited Findings
- Capital allocation is described as "a senior management team's most fundamental responsibility," with the objective of building long-term value per share; major uses of capital are organic reinvestment (capex, R&D, working capital), M&A, and returning cash to capital providers via dividends, buybacks, or debt paydown — [Morgan Stanley/Counterpoint Global: Capital Allocation (Mauboussin)](https://www.morganstanley.com/im/publication/insights/articles/article_capitalallocation.pdf); [HedgeFundAlpha: Michael Mauboussin on Capital Allocation](https://hedgefundalpha.com/education/michael-mauboussin-on-capital-allocation-updated/)
- Mauboussin's ROIC framework: the correlation between a firm's economic spread (**ROIC − WACC**) and its EV/Invested-Capital multiple is approximately **0.58** — i.e., the market materially rewards firms that earn returns above their cost of capital, and this spread is the central lens for judging whether reinvestment (capex/R&D) creates value. When ROIC is similar to WACC, growth "does not matter much" for value creation; when the spread is a large positive, growth is highly value-accretive; when **ROIC < WACC, every dollar of growth burns intrinsic value** — [Matthew Harbaugh: Inside Michael Mauboussin's ROIC Framework](https://matthewharbaugh.substack.com/p/inside-michael-mauboussins-roic-framework); [That Global Gambit: The Value Investor's Engine](https://thatglobalgambit.substack.com/p/the-value-investors-engine-a-review)
- On buybacks specifically: "In a buyback, selling shareholders benefit at the expense of ongoing shareholders if the stock is overvalued, and ongoing shareholders benefit at the expense of selling shareholders if the stock is undervalued" — meaning the checkable test for buyback quality is comparing the repurchase price to a reasonable estimate of intrinsic value at the time of the buyback (e.g., did the company buy more aggressively when its P/E or EV/EBITDA was depressed, or did buybacks continue/accelerate near all-time-high valuations) — [Matthew Harbaugh: Mauboussin ROIC Framework](https://matthewharbaugh.substack.com/p/inside-michael-mauboussins-roic-framework)
- Mauboussin's "Five Principles of Good Capital Allocation" (from his 2014 Journal of Applied Corporate Finance paper, as summarized by a secondary source): (1) **zero-based capital allocation** — re-justify the current allocation every year rather than assuming the status quo is right; (2) **fund strategies, not projects** — evaluate capital requests in the context of the larger strategy, since a project can pass a standalone rate-of-return hurdle but fail to serve the strategy (or vice versa); (3) **no formal capital rationing, but require sufficient returns** — don't treat internally generated cash as "free," still apply a cost-of-capital hurdle; (4) **zero tolerance for bad growth** — cut losses on failing initiatives rather than continuing to fund them (new products/businesses fail at a high rate); (5) **know value versus price for all businesses/assets owned** — understand when a business segment or the whole company is overvalued or undervalued by the market, and act (divest, buy back stock, etc.) when the gap is large enough to overcome transaction frictions — [Acquirer's Multiple: Michael Mauboussin — 5 Principles of Good Capital Allocation](https://acquirersmultiple.com/2022/12/michael-mauboussin-5-principles-of-good-capital-allocation/)
- Goodwill impairment as the concrete M&A red flag: "Large goodwill impairment charges signal overpayment in M&A or acquired business underperformance" and represent "an admission that a specific M&A decision by specific management destroyed billions of dollars in shareholder capital." A **single** write-down may reflect bad luck or a changed environment, but **multiple impairments across different deals within a few years is a much stronger signal** that the company "systematically overpays, overestimates synergies, or can't integrate what it buys" — one source frames a concrete checkable rule: "a company writing down three or more separate acquisitions inside five years has a broken M&A process" — [New Constructs: Too Much Goodwill — A Red Flag For Your Portfolio](https://www.newconstructs.com/much-goodwill-red-flag-portfolio/); [CT Acquisitions: Goodwill Impairment](https://ctacquisitions.com/goodwill-impairment/)
- Historical examples cited of goodwill write-downs as a cautionary pattern: AOL Time Warner ($54B), Microsoft/Nokia ($7.6B), Kraft Heinz ($15.4B) — [CT Acquisitions: Goodwill Impairment](https://ctacquisitions.com/goodwill-impairment/)

### Inferences
- A per-ticker checklist item should pull 5–10 years of: (a) ROIC vs. WACC trend (is the spread positive and stable or growing?); (b) buyback pace relative to the stock's own valuation history (did buybacks increase when P/E or EV/EBITDA was below its 5-year average, or did they continue/accelerate at valuation peaks?); (c) a count of goodwill impairment events and their cumulative size relative to total goodwill/market cap over the past 5–10 years (0–1 minor impairment = acceptable; 2+ meaningful impairments = red flag on M&A discipline); (d) whether debt paydown vs. dividend/buyback timing lines up with the balance-sheet/cycle context (e.g., paying down debt before a downturn vs. loading up on debt to fund buybacks at a cycle peak).
- The "five principles" give a qualitative lens (strategy-level thinking, discipline on bad growth, value-vs-price awareness) but are not themselves a numeric checklist — the quantitative anchors are the ROIC-WACC spread and the goodwill-impairment count/size, which are directly computable from financial statements.

## Key Question 3: How is "guidance beat/miss track record" tracked, and what counts as a good track record?

### Takeaway
A good guidance track record is a company that **consistently meets or modestly beats its own guidance** rather than one that posts large, erratic beats or misses; large consistent beats are often a red flag for deliberate **"sandbagging"** (lowballing guidance to guarantee a beat), a pattern sophisticated investors and analysts eventually price out once recognized, while large misses — especially repeated ones — signal poor forecasting/operational visibility.

### Cited Findings
- "Sandbagging" is defined as a deliberate tactic where companies issue guidance conservatively low — in some characterizations **15–25% below likely actual performance** — specifically to guarantee beating the target and generate a large "beat" narrative — [Epic Side Gigs: Sandbagging — How Companies Can Manipulate Forward Guidance](https://epicsidegigs.com/blog/sandbagging-how-companies-can-manipulate-forward-guidance/); [Pomegra: Sandbagging Explained](https://pomegra.io/learn/library/track-b-stock-market-core/earnings/chapter-06-guidance/sandbagging-explained)
- "Beat and raise" (beating the current quarter's guidance **and** raising forward guidance) is described as the rarest outcome among the standard earnings-reaction taxonomy, occurring in roughly **15–20%** of earnings reports, and triggers the strongest positive stock reactions — but a company can achieve beat-and-raise purely through conservative initial guidance (sandbagging) rather than genuine operational outperformance, so the pattern must be checked against guidance-setting behavior, not just the beat itself — [Pomegra: The Beat and Raise Pattern](https://pomegra.io/learn/library/track-b-stock-market-core/earnings/chapter-06-guidance/guidance-beat-and-raise)
- Market adaptation to lowballing: "investors have grown wise to this, which is why simply nudging past a lowballed estimate often produces little or no [stock price] reaction" — once analysts recognize a pattern of sandbagging, they adjust their own estimates above the company's formal guidance, which erodes the stock-price benefit of future beats — [Pomegra: Raising the Outlook](https://pomegra.io/learn/library/track-b-stock-market-core/earnings/chapter-06-guidance/raising-the-outlook); [CLS Blue Sky Blog: Does Lowball Guidance Work?](https://clsbluesky.law.columbia.edu/2023/07/07/does-lowball-guidance-work/?amp=1)
- Academic finding (as summarized by CLS Blue Sky Blog, a Columbia Law School blog covering corporate/financial law and securities scholarship): firms that use lowball guidance tend to see higher abnormal returns **only in the year the lowballing starts**, not in subsequent years — i.e., the market-reaction benefit of sandbagging dissipates once the pattern is recognized, implying investors should specifically check whether a company's "beat" history is *recent and escalating* (suspect) versus a *long-run steady small-beat pattern* (healthier) — [CLS Blue Sky Blog: Does Lowball Guidance Work?](https://clsbluesky.law.columbia.edu/2023/07/07/does-lowball-guidance-work/?amp=1)

### Inferences
- A practical per-ticker check: pull the last 8–12 quarters of (a) company-issued guidance range, (b) actual reported result, and (c) consensus analyst estimate at the time. A "good" track record = small, consistent beats (actual modestly above the high end of guidance) with guidance ranges that narrow/tighten appropriately as the quarter progresses, and with **infrequent, small misses** rather than occasional large misses. A "concerning" pattern = very large beats every quarter (likely sandbagging, especially if guidance keeps getting set well below where consensus already sits) or a recent shift from consistent beats to a miss (potential inflection in business fundamentals).
- Because beat-and-raise can mask sandbagging, cross-check it against the *magnitude* of the beat relative to the company's own historical beat size — a sudden widening of the beat margin without a clear fundamental catalyst is itself a flag worth investigating rather than a pure positive.

### Gaps
- No single named "track record scoring" methodology (e.g., a specific analyst framework scoring "X beats out of Y quarters = good") was found in the sourced material; the framework above is inferred from the sandbagging/beat-and-raise literature rather than a single named standard.

## Key Question 4: What is "skin in the game" / insider ownership percentage, and what level is considered meaningfully aligned vs. misaligned?

### Takeaway
There is no single universal cutoff, but a commonly cited range frames insider ownership as following a U-shaped alignment curve: alignment benefits rise as ownership climbs from very low levels, then flatten or reverse in a "entrenchment" zone (roughly 5–25% in one formulation), before rising again at very high concentration; in practice, most practitioner commentary converges on **roughly 5–15% as a "sweet spot"** for diversified companies and **10%+ (often 10–30%) for founder-led companies** as the threshold for "meaningful alignment."

### Cited Findings
- One academic-style ownership/value framework (as summarized by a secondary source): below 5% insider ownership, firm value rises with insider stakes ("alignment effect"); between roughly 5–25%, value tends to decline as managers become entrenched and can pursue personal interests with reduced risk of being disciplined by the market; above 25%, value rises again as insiders' interests become so large they fully align with all shareholders' interests — [UpsideInvest: How to Find Stocks With High Insider Ownership](https://www.upsideinvest.io/blog/how-to-find-stocks-with-high-insider-ownership)
- Practitioner framing: "5–15% represents the sweet spot for most companies — enough alignment without entrenchment," while for founder-led mid-cap companies specifically, **10% to 30%** insider ownership "can signal strong alignment" — [UpsideInvest: How to Find Stocks With High Insider Ownership](https://www.upsideinvest.io/blog/how-to-find-stocks-with-high-insider-ownership)
- Observational claim: "In smaller company portfolios, over half of holdings have a founder still involved and in nearly three quarters the executives own more than 5%," and that "academic studies consistently find statistical evidence for the outperformance of companies with large insider ownership, particularly when still led by their founders" — [UpsideInvest: How to Find Stocks With High Insider Ownership](https://www.upsideinvest.io/blog/how-to-find-stocks-with-high-insider-ownership)
- "Skin in the game" is framed by an asset manager (Oldfield Partners) as a qualitative alignment-of-interest concept between owners and managers — the specific numeric thresholds in that source were not surfaced in the search snippet, only the conceptual framing — [Oldfield Partners: Skin in the Game](https://oldfieldpartners.com/publications/skin-in-the-game/)

### Inferences
- A per-ticker checklist item: pull insider + 10% owner beneficial ownership percentage from the latest proxy (DEF 14A) or Form 3/4/5 cumulative holdings. Flag as "meaningfully aligned" if ≥10% (or ≥5% at minimum) and especially if concentrated in a founder/CEO who is still operationally active; flag as "potentially misaligned" if insider ownership is very low (<1–2%, i.e., management holds options/RSUs but little real capital at risk) or if ownership sits in the "entrenchment zone" (large block, e.g., >25-30%, held by a founder/family with dual-class voting structures that insulate them from any market discipline regardless of performance — a nuance about misalignment risk at very high concentration that is implied by the U-shape but not fully detailed in the sourced material).

### Gaps
- The "U-shaped" value curve (5%, 25% breakpoints) is from a single secondary-source summary without a named academic study or primary citation — treat the specific breakpoints (5%, 25%) as illustrative/approximate rather than a settled academic consensus.
- Could not find sourced material specifically distinguishing "aligned high ownership" from "misaligned entrenched high ownership" (e.g., via dual-class share structures) — this risk is a reasonable inference but not directly confirmed by a cited source in this research pass.

## Key Question 5: Exact definitions/formulas for SMA, EMA, golden/death cross, extension from 200-day MA, support/resistance, volume confirmation, RSI, ATR, and named frameworks (Bollinger, Wilder, Dow Theory, Weinstein)

### Takeaway
All the core technical constructs have exact, standardized formulas (SMA, EMA with α=2/(N+1), RSI via Wilder's smoothed 14-period average gain/loss, ATR via Wilder's smoothed 14-period true range) and widely cited numeric thresholds (RSI 70/30, 50/200-day golden/death cross, Bollinger Bands at 20-day SMA ± 2 standard deviations), while "overextension" from the 200-day MA and "support/resistance" are more subjective, practitioner-defined thresholds without one universal standard.

### Cited Findings
**Moving averages:**
- A **Simple Moving Average (SMA)** over N periods is the unweighted arithmetic mean of the closing price over the trailing N periods (standard, not explicitly re-derived in a dedicated source this pass since it's foundational, but confirmed implicitly across all MA sources, e.g. the Bollinger Bands and golden-cross sources below which define the "middle band"/moving average as such) — [StockCharts: Bollinger Bands](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/technical-indicators-and-overlays/technical-overlays/bollinger-bands)
- An **Exponential Moving Average (EMA)** is a weighted average where today's close is weighted by a smoothing factor **α = 2/(N+1)**, and the recursive formula is: **EMA(today) = α × Close(today) + (1 − α) × EMA(yesterday)**, equivalently **EMA(i) = EMA(i−1) + α × [Price(i) − EMA(i−1)]**. Because recent prices are weighted more heavily than older ones, EMA responds faster to new price action than an SMA of the same length — [user42.tuxfamily.org: Exponential Moving Average](https://user42.tuxfamily.org/chart/manual/Exponential-Moving-Average.html); [EMAIndicator.com: EMA Formula](https://emaindicator.com/blog/exponential-moving-average-formula/)
- **Golden cross**: the 50-day moving average crosses **above** the 200-day moving average, read as bullish — signaling the start/confirmation of a longer-term uptrend. **Death cross**: the 50-day MA crosses **below** the 200-day MA, read as bearish — a possible shift from uptrend to downtrend. Rationale for the 50/200 pairing: ~50 trading days approximates one quarter, ~200 trading days approximates one year, so the crossover reflects quarterly momentum relative to the annual trend — [SoFi: Death Cross Pattern](https://www.sofi.com/learn/content/death-cross-chart-pattern/); [Britannica: Death Cross vs. Golden Cross](https://www.britannica.com/money/golden-cross-vs-death-cross); [Charan Invests: Moving Averages Explained](https://www.charaninvests.com/blog/moving-averages-explained)

**Extension from the 200-day MA:**
- Calculation: **% extension = (Price ÷ 200-day SMA) − 1**. Worked example from source: price $66 vs. 200-day SMA $60 → (66/60) − 1 = 0.10 = **+10% extended** — [JustTheMarket: The 50-Day and 200-Day Moving Averages](https://justthemarket.com/metrics/moving-averages-50-and-200-day/)
- No single universal numeric threshold exists for "overextended" at the individual-stock level; cited practitioner-specific thresholds vary widely: trader **Dan Zanger** reportedly considers **20%** above the 200-day SMA (daily timeframe) as overextended, while trader **Oliver Kell** reportedly uses a much more aggressive **140%** above the 200-day MA as his overextension threshold (daily timeframe) — [Sakata's Substack: This is how I define an over-extended stock](https://sakatas.substack.com/p/this-is-how-i-define-an-over-extended)
- At the market-breadth level (percent of index constituents above their 200-day MA, a related but distinct metric), **≥70%** of stocks above their 200-day MA is characterized as "over-exuberance" that can precede a short-term pullback, and **>80%** is characterized as an overextended market where traders might reduce risk — [Enlightened Stock Trading: Percentage of Stocks Above Moving Average](https://enlightenedstocktrading.com/percentage-of-stocks-above-moving-average/); [Barchart: % of Stocks Above 200-Day Average](https://www.barchart.com/stocks/quotes/$MMTH/technical-analysis)

**Support/resistance:**
- Support and resistance are commonly identified via **prior swing highs and lows** (pivot points) — price levels where a prior reversal occurred — treated as zones rather than exact lines — [TradingView: Swing High/Low Support Resistance indicator description](https://www.tradingview.com/script/RBgMJvsB-Swing-High-Low-Support-Resistance/)
- **Round numbers** are frequently cited as significant support/resistance levels because they are psychologically memorable and attract clustered buy/sell orders — [QuantStrategy.io: Identifying True Support and Resistance Levels](https://quantstrategy.io/blog/identifying-true-support-and-resistance-levels-using-order/)
- **Volume-at-price clusters**: a price zone with historically high trading volume indicates a large concentration of market participants' cost basis at that level, which tends to act as support (on the way down, since many holders are "even" there and reluctant to sell at a loss, or eager to buy back at breakeven) or resistance (on the way up, since underwater holders look to exit at breakeven) — [TradingView: Volume Cluster Support & Resistance indicator description](https://www.tradingview.com/script/r4kYAMu4-Volume-Cluster-Support-Resistance/)

**Volume confirmation logic:**
- **Price up + rising volume** = healthy trend: strong conviction, broad participant agreement that the stock is worth more at the higher price — [ChartingLens: Volume Analysis](https://chartinglens.com/blog/volume-analysis-trading); [Wealthsimple: Trading volume — how to confirm trends and spot reversals](https://www.wealthsimple.com/en-ca/learn/volume-analysis)
- **Price up + falling volume** = weak/suspect trend, sometimes labeled "distribution," potentially indicating fewer participants willing to buy at higher prices and/or institutional selling into the rally — [ChartingLens: Volume Analysis](https://chartinglens.com/blog/volume-analysis-trading)
- **Breakdown on heavy volume** = "real" distribution — sellers decisively in control, more likely to represent a genuine trend reversal; one source defines a formal "Distribution Day" as a day where volume rises vs. the prior day **and** the close is lower, or where the stock trends up but shows little price progress on rising volume — [ChartingLens: Volume Analysis](https://chartinglens.com/blog/volume-analysis-trading)
- This is consistent with classical **Dow Theory**: volume should expand in the direction of the primary trend to confirm it (rising volume on up moves confirms an uptrend); if volume fails to expand in the direction of the trend, that is read as a warning sign that the trend lacks conviction and may not be valid — [StockCharts: Dow Theory](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/market-analysis/dow-theory); [TradeShaala: Six Basic Tenets of Dow Theory](https://tradesmartonline.in/tradeshaala/chapter/tenets-of-the-dow-theory/)

**RSI (Relative Strength Index) — J. Welles Wilder, originated in *New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems* (1978):**
- Formula: **RS = Average Gain ÷ Average Loss**; **RSI = 100 − [100 ÷ (1 + RS)]**
- Wilder's smoothing method (two-stage): first Average Gain = sum of gains over the first 14 periods ÷ 14; first Average Loss = sum of losses over the first 14 periods ÷ 14. Thereafter: **Average Gain = [(prior Average Gain × 13) + current period's Gain] ÷ 14**; **Average Loss = [(prior Average Loss × 13) + current period's Loss] ÷ 14** (this is Wilder's smoothing, mathematically a specific form of exponential smoothing)
- Default look-back period: **14** (days/periods). Standard thresholds: **RSI > 70 = overbought**, **RSI < 30 = oversold**, chosen by Wilder via back-testing on commodity markets as zones where price moves historically showed exhaustion
- Important caveat, explicitly noted in sourced material: Wilder described 70/30 as **guidelines, not absolute triggers** — in strong trending markets, RSI can remain above 70 (or below 30) for extended periods without reversing, so overbought/oversold readings "work best when prices move sideways within a range" and are less reliable in strongly trending markets — [StockCharts: RSI (ChartSchool)](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/technical-indicators-and-overlays/technical-indicators/relative-strength-index-rsi); [LiteFinance: RSI Indicator Guide](https://www.litefinance.org/blog/for-beginners/best-technical-indicators/rsi-relative-strength-index/)

**ATR (Average True Range) — also J. Welles Wilder, same 1978 book:**
- **True Range (TR)** for a given period = the greatest of: (Current High − Current Low), |Current High − Previous Close|, |Current Low − Previous Close|. In notation: **TR_t = max[(H_t − L_t), |H_t − C_(t-1)|, |L_t − C_(t-1)|]**
- Wilder's smoothed ATR: **Current ATR = [(Prior ATR × 13) + Current TR] ÷ 14**, with the first ATR value computed as the simple average of the first 14 TR readings (default period = 14, matching RSI's default) — [Fidelity: Average True Range](https://www.fidelity.com/bin-public/060_www_fidelity_com/documents/AverageTrueRange.pdf); [Wikipedia: Average true range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Average_true_range)
- ATR-based stop-loss sizing: for a long position, **Stop Loss = Entry Price − (ATR × Multiplier)**; for a short position, **Stop Loss = Entry Price + (ATR × Multiplier)**. The multiplier is a matter of trader risk tolerance, with **1.5 to 3× ATR commonly cited** as the typical range — this makes the stop distance self-adjusting to each stock's own current volatility rather than a fixed percentage — [Calculator Academy: ATR Stop Loss Calculator](https://calculator.academy/atr-average-true-range-stop-loss-calculator/); [IG: Average True Range Indicator](https://www.ig.com/en/trading-strategies/what-is-the-average-true-range--atr--indicator-and-how-do-you-tr-240905)

**Bollinger Bands — John Bollinger:**
- Standard construction (20, 2 setting): **Middle Band = 20-day SMA of closing price**; **Upper Band = 20-day SMA + (2 × 20-day standard deviation of closing price)**; **Lower Band = 20-day SMA − (2 × 20-day standard deviation of closing price)**. The standard deviation is computed over the same lookback period (20 days) as the SMA. Band width expands as volatility rises and contracts as volatility falls, since band distance is a direct function of the rolling standard deviation — [StockCharts: Bollinger Bands](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/technical-indicators-and-overlays/technical-overlays/bollinger-bands); [CMC Markets: Bollinger Bands Strategy & Formula](https://www.cmcmarkets.com/en-gb/technical-analysis/bollinger-bands)

**Dow Theory basics:**
- Markets move in three simultaneous trends: **primary** (the broad, long-term trend — described via the "tide" metaphor), **secondary** (intermediate corrections/rallies within the primary trend — "waves"), and **minor** (short-term day-to-day noise — "ripples") — [Dukascopy: Dow Theory](https://www.dukascopy.com/swiss/english/marketwatch/articles/dow-theory/); [TradeShaala: Six Basic Tenets of Dow Theory](https://tradesmartonline.in/tradeshaala/chapter/tenets-of-the-dow-theory/)
- Classic Dow Theory also required the Dow Jones Industrial Average and Dow Jones Transportation Average to **confirm** each other's new highs/lows for a trend signal to be valid (less directly relevant to single-stock analysis, but foundational to the trend-confirmation logic later applied to individual securities) — [StockCharts: Dow Theory](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/market-analysis/dow-theory)
- Volume is Dow Theory's "important but secondary" confirming factor: volume should expand in the direction of the primary trend; divergence (trend up, volume not expanding, or vice versa) is a warning sign — [TradeShaala: Six Basic Tenets of Dow Theory](https://tradesmartonline.in/tradeshaala/chapter/tenets-of-the-dow-theory/)

**Stan Weinstein's Stage Analysis (from *Secrets for Profiting in Bull and Bear Markets*):**
- Built around the **30-week Simple Moving Average** (weekly chart equivalent of a ~150-day daily SMA) as the organizing reference line, dividing a stock's price cycle into **four stages**: **Stage 1 (Basing)** — sideways action after a decline, with the 30-week MA flattening, signaling the downtrend has likely run its course; **Stage 2 (Advancing)** — price breaks above resistance and the 30-week MA turns up, the stage where new buying is favored; **Stage 3 (Topping)** — the 30-week MA flattens again, price action gets choppy, failed breakouts increase; **Stage 4 (Declining)** — price stays below a falling 30-week MA with lower highs and lower lows — [TradingMomentum: Stan Weinstein's Stage Analysis](https://tradingmomentum.substack.com/p/stan-weinsteins-stage-analysis-the); [Snappchart: Stan Weinstein Stage Analysis](https://www.snappchart.app/blog/technical-indicators/stan-weinstein-stage-analysis)
- A core Weinstein trading rule cited: stocks trading **beneath their 30-week moving average should never be considered for purchase**, especially when that average is declining — i.e., Stage Analysis is explicitly used as a **buy-timing/avoidance filter** layered on top of (not instead of) a fundamental view — [TradingMomentum: Stan Weinstein's Stage Analysis](https://tradingmomentum.substack.com/p/stan-weinsteins-stage-analysis-the); [DeepVue: Stan Weinstein Stage Analysis — Identify The Best Time To Buy And Sell](https://deepvue.com/indicators/stan-weinstein-stage-analysis-when-to-buy/)

### Inferences
- The exact formulas above (RSI, ATR, EMA, Bollinger Bands, % extension from 200-day MA) are all directly codeable into a checklist/spreadsheet or script with no ambiguity. The "overextension" and "support/resistance" concepts, by contrast, require the checklist to either (a) pick one practitioner convention explicitly (e.g., define "overextended" as >20–25% above the 200-day SMA, a middle-ground figure near Zanger's 20% threshold) and hold it constant for comparability across tickers, or (b) use the ATR-normalized version (extension measured in multiples of ATR rather than raw %) to adjust for the fact that a 20% extension is routine for a high-beta/high-ATR stock but extreme for a low-volatility one — this ATR-normalization approach is a reasonable synthesis but was not directly sourced from any single reference in this research pass.
- Weinstein's "never buy below a declining 30-week MA" rule maps directly onto the "golden/death cross" framework using a weekly rather than daily timeframe, and both are consistent with Dow Theory's trend-with-volume-confirmation logic — the three frameworks (Dow, Weinstein, golden/death cross) are different granularities of the same underlying principle: trade with the trend, and use volume to validate that the trend is real.

## Key Question 6: How do disciplined (especially value-oriented) investors use technicals only for entry timing / stop-loss, not as the core thesis?

### Takeaway
The common framing is a clean division of labor — **fundamentals answer "what to buy," technicals answer "when to buy/sell and how to size risk"** — with technical analysis treated as a secondary, execution-layer tool applied only after a fundamental thesis is established, and with value-oriented practice favoring a **fundamental ("thesis") stop** over a pure chart-based stop whenever the two conflict.

### Cited Findings
- "Technical analysis answers the question 'when to buy or sell' and is well suited for investors timing their entries and exits, while fundamental analysis answers 'what to buy.' For value investors, technical analysis can help identify the best entry and exit points, with fundamental analysis used first and technical analysis as a secondary consideration to optimize entry and exit points" — [Hard Money History: How Value Investing Can Benefit From Technical Analysis](https://www.hardmoneyhistory.com/value-investing-technical-analysis/)
- On stop-loss philosophy specifically: "For value investors, a fundamental stop is recommended — sell when the thesis breaks (such as when F-Score drops below 3, earnings yield turns negative, or debt becomes a problem), so the exit is tied to the reason you bought, not a chart line" — [Quant-Investing: Stop Loss Strategies Complete Guide](https://www.quant-investing.com/blog/stop-loss-strategies-complete-guide)
- On combining the two approaches in a repeatable process: "Combining fundamental growth filters with technical setup criteria helps narrow the universe of stocks, with a business expanding revenue and earnings providing fundamental tailwind, while a constructive chart improves entry timing and risk control. Fundamentals can define the bias, while technicals refine the exact timing and risk parameters" — [ChartMill: Alphabet — Strong Growth and High-Quality Technical Setup](https://www.chartmill.com/news/GOOGN/Chartmill-55873-Alphabet-Inc---GOOGL-6-14-051529-NASDAQGOOGN-Strong-Growth-and-a-High-Quality-Technical-Setup)

### Inferences
- A clean checklist structure for Step 6, consistent with this division of labor, is: (1) technicals are never used to generate the buy idea or override a fundamental red flag; (2) once a fundamental thesis (Steps 1–5) passes, technicals are used only to (a) time the entry (e.g., avoid buying deep in Weinstein Stage 4 / below a declining 200-day MA, prefer entries near support with volume confirmation, avoid chasing an RSI >70 / highly extended entry) and (b) size the stop-loss objectively via ATR multiples rather than an arbitrary percentage; (3) the stop itself should be the *tighter* (more conservative) of the technical stop (e.g., ATR-based or below a support level) and the fundamental "thesis-break" stop, since either kind of invalidation is a legitimate reason to exit.
